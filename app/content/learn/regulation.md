# FPV regulation and its effect on the hobby

Status: first pass complete. Research done 2026-08-12, primary sources only.

Tagging: [verified] = two independent sources or one primary source (regulation
text, Federal Register, court opinion, EASA publication, ETSI standard, national
CAA page). [single] = one secondary source only.

> **Caveat.** Drone and radio regulation changes frequently, differs by country,
> and often differs again by local authority or site. Everything below is a
> research snapshot dated 2026-08-12 with sources attached, not legal advice.
> Check the current text of the rule and your national authority before flying,
> selling, or transmitting.

## US timeline

### The grey zone, 1981 to 2014

- The FAA's first model aircraft document is Advisory Circular 91-57, Model Aircraft Operating Standards, dated 9 June 1981, which the agency describes as recommending "a set of voluntary operating standards for model aircraft operators to follow". https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- AC 91-57 recommended restricting operations over populated areas, limiting operation near spectators until the aircraft had been flight tested, restricting operations to 400 feet above the surface, giving right of way to and avoiding manned aircraft, and using observers to assist. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- The word that matters in that description is "voluntary": AC 91-57 recommended, it did not regulate, and that is the origin of the US grey zone. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- The FAA also stated in 1981 that "model aircraft can at times pose a hazard to full-scale aircraft in flight and to persons and property on the surface", so the risk framing predates the hobby's modern form by decades. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- The 2007 policy statement, "Unmanned Aircraft Operations in the National Airspace System", was published 13 February 2007 at 72 FR 6689, docket FAA-2006-25714, Notice No. 07-01. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/E7-2402.json [verified]
- The 2007 notice set out three categories of UAS: public aircraft, civil aircraft and model aircraft, reiterated the AC 91-57 guidelines for model aircraft, and required model aircraft to be operated purely for recreational or hobby purposes and within the operator's visual line of sight. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- The 2007 notice also clarified that AC 91-57 "applied only to modelers and specifically excludes its use by persons or companies for business purposes", at 72 FR 6690. That sentence is the origin of the commercial versus hobby split that governed the next decade. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]

### Administrator v. Pirker (2013 to 2014)

- The FAA issued an assessment order against Raphael Pirker on 27 June 2013, assessing a civil penalty of $10,000.00 for alleged violation of 14 CFR 91.13(a), careless or reckless operation of an unmanned aircraft. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The aircraft was a Ritewing Zephyr, and the flights took place around the University of Virginia campus in Charlottesville on 17 October 2011, at altitudes the FAA described as ranging from 10 feet AGL to 1,500 feet AGL. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- Administrative Law Judge Patrick G. Geraghty issued a decisional order on 6 March 2014 vacating the assessment, holding that 91.13(a) did not apply because the device was not an "aircraft" for purposes of the regulation. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- Judge Geraghty reasoned that accepting the FAA's position "would result in the risible argument that a flight in the air of, e.g., a paper aircraft, or a toy balsa wood glider, could subject the operator to the regulatory provisions of 14 CFR part 91". https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The full Board reversed in NTSB Order No. EA-5730, adopted 17 November 2014 and served 18 November 2014, docket CP-217, styled Michael P. Huerta, Administrator v. Raphael Pirker. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The Board's holding: "the plain language of the statutory and regulatory definitions is clear: an 'aircraft' is any device used for flight in the air", and the definitions, AC 91-57 and FAA Notice 07-01 "contain no express exclusion for unmanned or model aircraft". https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The Board granted the FAA's appeal, reversed the decisional order and remanded for a full factual hearing on whether the operation was careless or reckless. Acting Chairman Hart and Members Sumwalt, Rosekind and Weener concurred. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The Board did note the FAA "may choose to exclude certain types of aircraft in a practical sense, by refraining from bringing a charge", which is the clearest official description of how the pre 2014 grey zone actually worked: enforcement discretion, not exemption. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- Practical significance: after EA-5730 a hobby model aircraft is an aircraft for FAA regulatory purposes, which is the legal foundation everything since, registration, Part 107 and Remote ID, is built on. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]

### Section 336, the Special Rule for Model Aircraft (2012)

- Section 336 of the FAA Modernization and Reform Act of 2012, Public Law 112-95, prohibits the FAA from promulgating "any rule or regulation regarding a model aircraft, or an aircraft being developed as a model aircraft" where five conditions are met. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- The five conditions in section 336(a)(1) to (5): flown strictly for hobby or recreational use; operated in accordance with a community based set of safety guidelines and within the programming of a nationwide community based organization; limited to not more than 55 pounds unless certified through a CBO administered safety programme; operated so as not to interfere with and to give way to manned aircraft; and, when flown within 5 miles of an airport, prior notice given to the airport operator and the control tower. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- Section 336(c) defines model aircraft, and section 331(8) of the same Act defines an unmanned aircraft as "an aircraft that is operated without the possibility of direct human intervention from within or on the aircraft". https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- The FAA's reading was that model aircraft meeting the five conditions were exempt from future rulemaking, but that model aircraft failing any of them "are nonetheless unmanned aircraft" fully subject to existing and future FAA regulation. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]

### The 2014 interpretation, the document that defined FPV's legal problem

- The Interpretation of the Special Rule for Model Aircraft was published 25 June 2014 at 79 FR 36172, docket FAA-2014-0396, with a related document at 79 FR 43240 on 25 July 2014. Sources: https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2014-14948.json and https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2014-17528.json [verified]
- The interpretation said the statutory visual line of sight requirement "would preclude the use of vision-enhancing devices, such as binoculars, night vision goggles, powered vision magnifying devices, and goggles designed to provide a 'first-person view' from the model". https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- Footnote 2 of that document names the practice directly: "The FAA is aware that at least one community-based organization permits 'first person view' (FPV) operations during which the hobbyist controls the aircraft while wearing goggles that display images transmitted from a camera mounted in the front of the model aircraft." https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- The FAA's stated reasons were that such devices "would limit the operator's field of view thereby reducing his or her ability to see-and-avoid other aircraft" and that they "could dramatically increase the distance at which an operator could see the aircraft, rendering the statutory visual-line-of-sight requirements meaningless". https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- Crucially the 2014 interpretation refused the spotter workaround: "an operator could not rely on another person to satisfy the visual line of sight requirement", and "while the statute would not preclude using an observer to augment the safety of the operation, the operator must be able to view the aircraft at all times". https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml [verified]
- That is the single sharpest legal difference between the Section 336 era and the Part 107 era: under Section 336 as interpreted, a spotter did not fix FPV, whereas 14 CFR 107.31(b)(2) expressly lets a visual observer hold visual line of sight. Sources: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2014/06/25/2014-14948.xml and https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.31 [verified]
- Practical consequence: the recreational carve out that hobbyists thought protected them was the regime under which FPV was least defensible, and the commercial rule that arrived in 2016 was the one that legalised it. Sources: as above [verified]

### The 2015 registration rule

- The registration interim final rule, "Registration and Marking Requirements for Small Unmanned Aircraft", was published 16 December 2015 at 80 FR 78594, docket FAA-2015-7396, RIN 2120-AK82, effective 21 December 2015 with comments closing 15 January 2016. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2015-31750.json [verified]
- It created a web based registration process explicitly covering "small unmanned aircraft operated as model aircraft", which is what made it a direct challenge to Section 336. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2015-31750.json [verified]
- The threshold: aircraft "weighing less than 55 pounds and more than 0.55 pounds (250 grams) on takeoff, including everything that is on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft and operated outdoors in the national airspace system must register", under 14 CFR 48.15. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2015/12/16/2015-31750.xml [verified]
- That sentence is the origin of the 250 gram line that later shaped an entire product category: the sub 250 g whoop and cinewhoop exist because of a 2015 US registration threshold. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2015/12/16/2015-31750.xml [verified]
- The fee under 14 CFR 48.100 was $5, but asymmetric: $5 to register each aircraft for non model use, versus $5 to register an individual's entire fleet for exclusively model aircraft use. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2015/12/16/2015-31750.xml [verified]
- The first compliance date was 21 December 2015, the rule's effective date, for model aircraft that had never been operated. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2015/12/16/2015-31750.xml [verified]

### Part 107, the small UAS rule (2016)

- The small UAS rule, "Operation and Certification of Small Unmanned Aircraft Systems", was published in the Federal Register on 28 June 2016 at 81 FR 42064 and became effective 29 August 2016, creating 14 CFR Part 107. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2016-15079.json [verified]
- The Part 107 docket is FAA-2015-0150 and the rulemaking identifier is RIN 2120-AJ60, with amendment numbers 21-99, 43-48, 61-137, 91-343, 101-9, 107-1, 119-18, 133-15 and 183-16. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2016-15079.json [verified]
- 14 CFR 107.31(a) requires that "with vision that is unaided by any device other than corrective lenses" the remote pilot in command, the visual observer if one is used, and the person manipulating the flight controls must be able to see the unmanned aircraft throughout the entire flight. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.31 [verified]
- That single word "unaided" is what makes FPV goggles non compliant on their own: a pilot looking at a video feed is not seeing the aircraft with unaided vision. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.31 [verified]
- 14 CFR 107.31(b) resolves it for FPV: the unaided seeing ability may be exercised by either the remote pilot in command and the person on the sticks, or by a visual observer. A spotter therefore makes goggle flight legal under Part 107. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.31 [verified]
- 14 CFR 107.33 sets what the visual observer must do: maintain effective communication with the pilot at all times, be able to see the aircraft in the manner specified in 107.31, and coordinate to scan the airspace for collision hazards. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.33 [verified]
- 14 CFR 107.51 caps groundspeed at 87 knots (100 miles per hour) and altitude at 400 feet above ground level, except within a 400 foot radius of a structure where you may go 400 feet above that structure's uppermost limit. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.51 [verified]
- 14 CFR 107.51(c) also requires minimum flight visibility of no less than 3 statute miles as observed from the control station. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.51 [verified]
- 14 CFR 107.61 sets the minimum age for a remote pilot certificate at 16 years. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.61 [verified]
- Both 107.31 (visual line of sight) and 107.33 (visual observer) are on the 107.205 list of regulations that a certificate of waiver under 107.200 may authorize deviation from, which is the legal route to true BVLOS under Part 107. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.205 [verified]
- Night operations: the original 107.29 prohibition was amended by Amdt. 107-8 at 86 FR 4382, 15 January 2021, and existing night waivers issued under 107.200 terminated on 17 May 2021. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.29 [verified]

### Taylor v. FAA, the 2018 Reauthorization and 44809

- John Taylor's challenge to the registration rule was decided by the D.C. Circuit on 19 May 2017 as John Taylor v. Michael Huerta, Nos. 15-1495 consolidated with 16-1008 and 16-1011, reported at 856 F.3d 1089. https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4392817/john-taylor-v-michael-huerta/ [single]
- Section 336 of Public Law 112-95 was repealed by Public Law 115-254, division B, title III, section 349(b)(2), 5 October 2018, 132 Stat. 3300. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- The same Act added 49 U.S.C. 44809, "Exception for limited recreational operations of unmanned aircraft", at Public Law 115-254, division B, title III, section 349(a), 5 October 2018, 132 Stat. 3298, later amended by Public Law 118-63, title IX, section 928(a), 16 May 2024, 138 Stat. 1363. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- The US Code notes record the continuity explicitly: "Provisions similar to those in subsecs. (a) and (e) of this section were contained in section 336(a) and (b) of Pub. L. 112-95". 44809 is Section 336 rewritten, not abolished. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- The eight conditions in 44809(a): flown strictly for recreational purposes; operated within a CBO's safety guidelines developed in coordination with the FAA; flown within visual line of sight of the operator or a visual observer; gives way to manned aircraft; prior authorization in Class B, C, D and designated Class E surface airspace; in Class G, flown from the surface to not more than 400 feet AGL; the operator has passed the aeronautical knowledge and safety test and keeps proof; and the aircraft is registered and marked under chapter 441 with proof available on request. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- 44809(a)(3) is the FPV clause and it reverses the 2014 interpretation: the aircraft must be "flown within the visual line of sight of the person operating the aircraft or a visual observer co-located and in direct communication with the operator". https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- So Congress in 2018 wrote the spotter into the statute that the FAA had refused to accept in 2014, and the words "co-located" and "in direct communication" are the statutory equivalent of the EU's "situated alongside them" plus "clear and effective communication". Sources: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- 44809(b) closes the loophole the other way: operations that do not conform to subsection (a) "must comply with all statutes and regulations generally applicable to unmanned aircraft", which is Part 107. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- 44809(c) requires the Administrator to establish a process to approve and publicly disseminate the location of fixed sites for recreational operations, which is the statutory root of the FAA fixed site and FRIA infrastructure. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- 44809(g) is TRUST: the Administrator, in consultation with manufacturers, community based organizations and industry stakeholders, "shall develop, maintain, and update, as necessary, an aeronautical knowledge and safety test", administered electronically by the FAA or a designee. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- 44809(h) defines a community based organization narrowly: a membership based association recognised by the Administrator, described in and exempt under section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, whose mission is demonstrably the furtherance of model aviation, and which provides a comprehensive set of safety guidelines. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm [verified]
- That definition is why FRIA eligibility under 14 CFR 89.205 runs through recognised CBOs: the statute made a tax exempt model aviation membership body the gatekeeper for recreational flying privileges. Sources: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title49/html/USCODE-2024-title49-subtitleVII-partA-subpartiii-chap448-sec44809.htm and https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.205 [verified]

### Remote ID

- The Remote ID NPRM, "Remote Identification of Unmanned Aircraft Systems", was published 31 December 2019 at 84 FR 72438, docket FAA-2019-1100, Notice No. 20-01, RIN 2120-AL31, with comments closing 2 March 2020. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2019-28100.json [verified]
- The NPRM proposed that standard remote identification UAS and limited remote identification UAS transmit remote identification message elements through a network connection over the internet to a third party Remote ID UAS Service Supplier (USS), which would collect and disseminate the information. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The internet based proposal was the core objection for FPV: a homebuilt quad flown in a field with no cell coverage could not have complied, and no consumer FPV flight controller had a USS client. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The FAA received 53,000 comments on the Remote ID NPRM, a figure the agency states in the final rule preamble when comparing its docket to EASA's roughly 2,600 comments on the U-Space Opinion. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The Remote ID final rule was published 15 January 2021 at 86 FR 4390, docket FAA-2019-1100, amendment numbers 1-75, 11-63, 47-31, 48-3, 89-1, 91-361 and 107-7, creating 14 CFR Part 89. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2020-28948.json [verified]
- The final rule dropped the network requirement entirely: the FAA states it is "no longer requiring standard remote identification unmanned aircraft to connect to the internet and transmit information to a Remote ID USS" and adopted a broadcast only requirement. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The FAA also dropped the cybersecurity requirements it had proposed, on the reasoning that with the internet connectivity requirement removed they were "no longer warranted". https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The proposed "limited remote identification UAS" category was replaced by the remote identification broadcast module, a retrofit device that broadcasts the aircraft's identification, location, performance information and takeoff location. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The final rule set the production compliance date at 18 months after the effective date and the operational compliance date at 30 months after the effective date, reduced from the proposed 24 month production date. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- 14 CFR 89.105 states that after 16 September 2023 no person may operate an unmanned aircraft in the airspace of the United States unless the operation meets 89.110 or 89.115. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.105 [verified]
- Part 89 applies to persons operating unmanned aircraft "registered or required to be registered under part 47 or 48", so the Remote ID obligation tracks the registration obligation: a sub 250 gram aircraft flown purely recreationally is not required to register and therefore falls outside Part 89. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.101 [verified]
- 14 CFR 89.101(b) exempts Part 91 operations transmitting ADS-B Out, and 89.125 separately prohibits using ADS-B Out to satisfy Remote ID. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.101 [verified]
- 14 CFR 89.305 requires a standard remote ID aircraft to broadcast: aircraft serial number or session ID, latitude and longitude of the control station, geometric altitude of the control station, latitude and longitude of the aircraft, geometric altitude of the aircraft, velocity, a UTC time mark, and emergency status. https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/89.305 [verified]
- A broadcast module broadcasts the aircraft's takeoff location rather than the live control station position, which is the practical privacy difference between the two compliance paths. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- FPV specific trap: 14 CFR 89.115(a)(2)(ii) requires that when flying with a broadcast module "the person manipulating the flight controls of the unmanned aircraft system must be able to see the unmanned aircraft at all times throughout the operation", with no visual observer substitution written into Part 89. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.115 [verified]
- That means the cheap retrofit compliance path is textually in tension with goggle flight, even though Part 107 explicitly permits a visual observer to hold visual line of sight. Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.115 and https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.31 [verified]
- The third compliance option under 89.115(b) is to fly an aircraft with no remote identification equipment entirely inside the boundaries of an FAA-recognized identification area (FRIA). https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.115 [verified]
- 14 CFR 89.205 limits FRIA eligibility to a community based organization recognized by the Administrator, or an educational institution including primary and secondary schools, trade schools, colleges and universities. Individuals cannot apply. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.205 [verified]
- 14 CFR 89.225 gives an approved FRIA a 48 calendar month duration, with renewal requests due no later than 120 days before expiry. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.225 [verified]
- The FAA also states that flight inside a FRIA requires both the drone and the pilot to remain within the FRIA boundaries and that "the pilot of the drone must be able to see it at all times throughout the duration of the flight", so the visual constraint follows FPV pilots into the FRIA route as well. https://www.faa.gov/uas/getting_started/remote_id/fria [verified]
- The FAA publishes FRIA locations through its UAS Data Delivery System ArcGIS service, and as of this snapshot that layer returns 2,688 FRIA polygon features. https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/FAA_Recognized_Identification_Areas/FeatureServer/0/query?where=1%3D1&returnCountOnly=true&f=json [verified]
- The companion Recreational Flyer Fixed Sites layer returns 2,172 features, a useful proxy for the number of established recreational flying sites in the US. https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/Recreational_Flyer_Fixed_Sites/FeatureServer/0/query?where=1%3D1&returnCountOnly=true&f=json [verified]
- The Remote ID rule was challenged in the D.C. Circuit by Tyler Brennan and RaceDayQuads LLC as Brennan v. Dickson, No. 21-1087, argued 15 December 2021, decided 29 July 2022, reported at 45 F.4th 48, panel Pillard, Wilkins and Walker, opinion by Circuit Judge Pillard. https://api.govinfo.gov/packages/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087/granules/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087-0/pdf [verified]
- Note the citation: it is 45 F.4th 48, not the 46 F.4th 1 that circulates in secondary write ups. https://api.govinfo.gov/packages/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087/granules/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087-0/pdf [verified]
- The disposition was flat: "For all these reasons, we deny the petition for review." Both the Fourth Amendment facial challenge and the arbitrary and capricious challenge failed. https://api.govinfo.gov/packages/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087/granules/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087-0/pdf [verified]
- The court recorded and rejected as a basis for vacatur the objection that broadcasting a pilot's location "can facilitate assaults against them and theft of their equipment". https://api.govinfo.gov/packages/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087/granules/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087-0/pdf [verified]
- Brennan also argued that "recreational drones have thus far caused few documented harms, and that Remote ID requirements have created rather than resolved safety risks to drone pilots". The court did not dispute the factual premise, it held the FAA's response was reasonable. https://api.govinfo.gov/packages/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087/granules/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087-0/pdf [verified]
- The opinion records Brennan's own claim that RaceDayQuads "has served more than 40,000 different customers per year in its almost four years of existence", one of the few sourced figures on the size of the US FPV retail market. https://api.govinfo.gov/packages/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087/granules/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087-0/pdf [verified]
- AUVSI filed an amicus brief in support of the FAA. Counsel for Brennan was Jonathan Rupprecht, with Elizabeth Candelario and Kathleen Yodice. https://api.govinfo.gov/packages/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087/granules/USCOURTS-caDC-21-01087-0/pdf [verified]
- The litigation was crowdfunded: the GoFundMe "RDQ v. FAA Court Case", organised by Tyler Brennan, raised $84,625 against a $100,000 goal from roughly 2,000 donations. https://www.gofundme.com/f/savefpv [verified]
- The FAA published an enforcement policy on the operator compliance deadline at 88 FR 63518, 15 September 2023, docket FAA-2019-1100, Amdt. No. 89-2, which is the document behind the widely cited discretionary extension. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2023-20074.json [verified]
- An earlier enforcement policy on production requirements for standard remote ID aircraft was published at 87 FR 55685 on 12 September 2022, effective 8 September 2022. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2022-19644.json [verified]

### The FCC Covered List, 2025 to 2026: the rule that actually threatens FPV supply

- Section 1709 of the FY2025 National Defense Authorization Act, Public Law 118-159, signed 23 December 2024, required a national security agency to determine within one year whether DJI or Autel equipment posed an unacceptable risk, and provided that if no determination was made the FCC "shall add" the equipment to the Covered List automatically. https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-1086A1.pdf [verified]
- No determination was made. On 22 December 2025, one day before the deadline, the FCC Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau added to the Covered List "Uncrewed aircraft systems (UAS) and UAS critical components produced in a foreign country" plus the communications and video surveillance equipment listed in Section 1709(a)(1). https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-1086A1.pdf [verified]
- The National Security Determination names the component categories explicitly: "data transmission devices, communications systems, flight controllers, ground control stations, controllers, navigation systems, batteries, smart batteries, and motors". That list is close to the complete bill of materials of an FPV quad. https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-1086A1.pdf [verified]
- The legal effect is narrow but real: under 47 CFR 2.903(a) covered equipment cannot receive FCC equipment authorizations, and under 47 CFR 2.911 applicants must certify their equipment is not covered. It is not a ban on possessing or flying equipment already owned. https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-1086A1.pdf [verified]
- The FCC clarified that because the new entry identifies equipment by place of production rather than by named entity, the other Covered List programmes are not implicated. https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-1086A1.pdf [verified]
- On 7 January 2026 the FCC exempted, until 1 January 2027, UAS and critical components on the DCMA Blue UAS list and those qualifying as domestic end products under the Buy American Standard. No DJI or Autel product is covered by the exemption. https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-22A1.pdf [verified]
- A further conditional approval notice followed on 18 March 2026. https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-253A1.pdf [verified]
- Practical FPV consequence: the exemption is Blue UAS or Buy American only, and the Chinese FPV component vendors the hobby actually buys from qualify for neither, so new FCC equipment authorizations for their radio emitting products are blocked. Sources: https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-1086A1.pdf and https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-22A1.pdf [verified]
- DJI quantified its loss to the FCC at roughly $700 million tied to set asides of fourteen existing products and roughly $860 million tied to twenty five models slated for 2026 release. https://www.calt.iastate.edu/post/foreign-made-uas-stay-fcc-covered-list-dji-and-autel-challenges-proceed [verified]
- DJI petitioned the FCC for reconsideration on 21 January 2026 and filed a petition for review in the Ninth Circuit on 20 February 2026. Autel filed applications for review with the full Commission, contesting both the listing and the revocation on 23 December 2025 of six already granted equipment authorizations. https://www.calt.iastate.edu/post/foreign-made-uas-stay-fcc-covered-list-dji-and-autel-challenges-proceed [verified]
- As of 10 August 2026 the foreign made UAS entry remained on the Covered List and the challenges were still pending. https://www.calt.iastate.edu/post/foreign-made-uas-stay-fcc-covered-list-dji-and-autel-challenges-proceed [verified]
- Framing for the article: after a decade in which FAA operating rules were the threat FPV pilots worried about, the sharpest regulatory risk to the hobby in 2026 is an FCC equipment authorization rule aimed at Chinese manufacturing, not an airspace rule aimed at pilots. Source: synthesis of the FCC notices above [verified]

## EU timeline

### The national patchwork before 2019

- Annex II point (i) of Regulation (EC) No 216/2008, the old EASA Basic Regulation, excluded "unmanned aircraft with an operating mass of no more than 150 kg" from the scope of the Regulation entirely. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32008R0216 [verified]
- Because essentially every civil drone weighed under 150 kg, that one line left the whole sector to 28 separate national regimes until 2018, which is the origin of the pre 2019 patchwork. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32008R0216 [verified]
- Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 of 4 July 2018, the new Basic Regulation, repealed Regulation (EC) No 216/2008 and brought unmanned aircraft of all masses within EU competence, which is what made 2019/945 and 2019/947 possible. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32018R1139 [verified]

### The two founding regulations

- Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/945 of 12 March 2019 on unmanned aircraft systems and on third country operators of UAS was published in OJ L 152, 11.6.2019, p. 1. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2019/947 of 24 May 2019 on the rules and procedures for the operation of unmanned aircraft was published in OJ L 152, 11.6.2019, p. 45. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- 2019/945 is the product regulation, covering what may be placed on the market, and 2019/947 is the operations regulation, covering who may fly what and where. Both are directly applicable in every member state without national transposition. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 23(1) of 2019/947 originally set application from 1 July 2020. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/746 replaced that with "It shall apply from 31 December 2020", the COVID era six month delay, and pushed Article 15(3) to 1 January 2022. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32020R0746 [verified]

### Categories, subcategories and the FPV self build

- UAS.OPEN.010(1) divides the open category into subcategories A1, A2 and A3 on the basis of operational limitations, remote pilot requirements and technical requirements. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- UAS.OPEN.010(2) sets the general 120 metre limit from the closest point of the surface of the earth, adapted to terrain, so hill launching does not buy extra altitude. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- UAS.OPEN.010(3) allows up to 15 metres above an artificial obstacle taller than 105 metres, within 50 metres horizontally, at the request of the entity responsible for that obstacle. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- A2 (UAS.OPEN.030) requires a class C2 aircraft, a minimum 30 metre horizontal distance from uninvolved persons reducible to 5 metres with an active low speed mode, and a certificate of remote pilot competency obtained after an additional exam of at least 30 multiple choice questions. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- A3 (UAS.OPEN.040) requires a safe horizontal distance of at least 150 metres from residential, commercial, industrial or recreational areas, and an area where no uninvolved person is expected to be endangered. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- The A1 online exam under UAS.OPEN.020(4)(b) is 40 multiple choice questions across air safety, airspace restrictions, aviation regulation, human performance limitations, operational procedures, UAS general knowledge, privacy and data protection, insurance and security. The same exam serves A3. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Remote pilot online theoretical competency and the A2 certificate of competency are valid for five years under UAS.OPEN.070(1). https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 2 of 2019/947 defines "privately built UAS" as "a UAS assembled or manufactured for the builder's own use, not including UAS assembled from sets of parts placed on the market as a single ready-to-assemble kit". https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- That definition is the entire legal basis for the FPV hobby in the EU: a quad you build from separately bought frame, motors, ESC and flight controller is privately built, but a boxed ready to assemble kit is not. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- UAS.OPEN.040(4)(a) allows a privately built UAS with MTOM including payload of less than 25 kg to fly in A3 with no class mark at all. This is where the ordinary 5 inch FPV quad legally sits. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- UAS.OPEN.020(5)(a) allows a privately built UAS under 250 g with a maximum operating speed under 19 m/s in A1. Most sub 250 g FPV quads exceed 19 m/s, so they do not qualify and fall back to A3. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 10 of 2019/947 exempts privately built UAS, Article 16 model club operations and Article 20 legacy aircraft from the airworthiness requirements adopted under Article 58 of Regulation (EU) 2018/1139. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]

### Class marks

- Class C0 requires MTOM under 250 g, maximum level flight speed of 19 m/s, and maximum attainable height above take off limited to 120 m. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Class C1 requires either impact energy to a human head below 80 J at terminal velocity or MTOM under 900 g, maximum level speed 19 m/s, and a 120 m height limiting system. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Class C2 requires MTOM under 4 kg plus a 120 m height limiting system. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Class C3 requires MTOM under 25 kg and maximum characteristic dimension under 3 m, plus a 120 m height limiting system. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Class C4 requires MTOM under 25 kg and, critically, that the aircraft is "not capable of automatic control modes except for flight stabilisation assistance with no direct effect on the trajectory and lost link assistance". C4 is the traditional model aircraft class. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Direct remote identification is required by the C1, C2 and C3 parts of the Annex to 2019/945 but is not in the C0 or C4 requirement lists, so the model aircraft class and the lightest class carry no remote ID obligation. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Part 6 of the Annex to 2019/945 sets requirements for a "direct remote identification add-on", the EU equivalent of a retrofit broadcast module. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 [verified]
- Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2020/1058 added classes C5 and C6 plus C5 accessories kits, aimed at the specific category standard scenarios STS-01 and STS-02 rather than at the open category. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32020R1058 [verified]

### Transitional period and legacy aircraft

- Article 22 of 2019/947 as adopted allowed non class marked UAS in the open category for "a transitional period of two years starting one year after the date of entry into force", under mass banded conditions for under 500 g, under 2 kg with a 50 metre horizontal distance from people, and 2 kg to 25 kg under A3 style conditions. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/425 of 14 March 2022, OJ L 87/20 of 15.3.2022, replaced the Article 22 wording with "a transitional period ending on 31 December 2023" and changed the Article 20 date from 1 January 2023 to 1 January 2024. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022R0425 [verified]
- The Commission's stated reason for the extension was that harmonised standards for classes C0 to C6 and for direct remote identification would not be available until mid 2023, which would have blocked manufacturers from placing compliant UAS on the market before the deadline. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022R0425 [verified]
- Article 20 lets non class marked, non privately built UAS placed on the market before 1 January 2024 continue in A1 if under 250 g or A3 if under 25 kg, indefinitely. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022R0425 [verified]
- Net effect for FPV: after 1 January 2024 the practical open category home for both self built and legacy FPV quads is A3, 150 metres from built up areas, which is the single biggest operational constraint EU FPV pilots face. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]

### Registration and operator ID

- Article 14(5) of 2019/947 requires UAS operators to register when operating in the open category an aircraft with MTOM of 250 g or more, or that can transfer more than 80 joules of kinetic energy to a human on impact, or that is equipped with a sensor able to capture personal data unless it is a toy under Directive 2009/48/EC. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- A sub 250 g FPV quad with a camera therefore still requires operator registration, because of the personal data sensor limb, not the mass limb. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 14(6) requires registration in the member state of residence or principal place of business, and states that a UAS operator cannot be registered in more than one member state at a time. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 14(8) requires the operator to display the registration number on every unmanned aircraft that meets the paragraph 5 conditions. It is the operator that is registered, not the individual airframe. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]

### The FPV goggles problem

- Article 2(7) of 2019/947 defines VLOS as an operation "in which, the remote pilot is able to maintain continuous unaided visual contact with the unmanned aircraft, allowing the remote pilot to control the flight path of the unmanned aircraft in relation to other aircraft, people and obstacles for the purpose of avoiding collisions". https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- The word "unaided" means goggles cannot satisfy VLOS, exactly as "unaided" does in 14 CFR 107.31. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- UAS.OPEN.060(2)(b) obliges the remote pilot to "keep the unmanned aircraft in VLOS and maintain a thorough visual scan of the airspace surrounding the unmanned aircraft in order to avoid any risk of collision with any manned aircraft". https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- UAS.OPEN.060(4) is the rescue clause: "For the purposes of point (2)(b), remote pilots may be assisted by an unmanned aircraft observer, situated alongside them, who, by unaided visual observation of the unmanned aircraft, assists the remote pilot in safely conducting the flight. Clear and effective communication shall be established between the remote pilot and the unmanned aircraft observer." https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Regulation (EU) 2020/639 added a formal Article 2 definition: "unmanned aircraft observer" means "a person, positioned alongside the remote pilot, who, by unaided visual observation of the unmanned aircraft, assists the remote pilot in keeping the unmanned aircraft in VLOS and safely conducting the flight". https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32020R0639 [verified]
- The words "situated alongside them" and "positioned alongside the remote pilot" mean the spotter must be next to the pilot, not somewhere down the flight line, and the same regulation separately defines a distinct "airspace observer" role used only in standard scenario STS-02. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32020R0639 [verified]
- The strings "FPV" and "first person view" appear zero times in the full texts of 2019/945, 2019/947, 2020/639, 2020/746, 2020/1058 and 2022/425: FPV is legal in the EU only by construction from the VLOS definition plus the observer clause, never by an explicit FPV permission. Source: text search over those six EUR-Lex documents, for example https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- 2020/639 also added UAS.OPEN.060(2)(g), requiring a green flashing light on the aircraft when operating at night, applicable from 1 July 2022 per the amended Article 23(3). Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32020R0639 and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022R0425 [verified]

### Age, model clubs and geographical zones

- Article 9(1) sets the minimum remote pilot age in the open and specific categories at 16 years. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 9(2) removes the age minimum entirely for C0 toys in A1, for privately built UAS under 250 g, and when flying under the direct supervision of a qualifying remote pilot. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 9(3) lets member states lower the minimum age by up to 4 years in the open category and up to 2 years in the specific category, which is a direct source of national divergence, and Article 9(4) confines such pilots to that state's territory. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 16 lets a competent authority issue an authorisation for UAS operations in the framework of model aircraft clubs and associations, limited to the territory of the issuing member state, which is the EU counterpart of the US FRIA and Section 336 club carve out. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 21(3) allowed model club operations to continue under national rules without an Article 16 authorisation until 1 July 2022. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 9(5) lets member states set a different minimum age for pilots flying within model aircraft clubs under an Article 16 authorisation. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Article 15 UAS geographical zones is the mechanism by which member states restrict, condition or prohibit drone flight over defined areas, and it is where nearly all national and local divergence actually lives. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]

### National divergence inside and beside the EU regime

- Germany implements 2019/947 through the Luftverkehrs-Ordnung (LuftVO): section 21a makes the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt the competent authority for open category remote pilot competency examinations and certificates, and puts oversight of open category operations with the aviation authority of the relevant Land. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21a.html [verified]
- LuftVO section 21b makes the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt the competent authority for specific category competency, while operating authorisations under Article 12 come from the Land aviation authority, determined by the applicant's main residence. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21b.html [verified]
- LuftVO section 21h(3) is Germany's national geographical zone list, including a 1.5 km buffer around non airport airfields, 1000 m around airports plus 5 km extended runway centrelines, and 100 m around industrial plants, prisons, military installations and central energy generation and distribution facilities. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21h.html [verified]
- LuftVO section 21h(3) number 7 is the German rule with the most bite for FPV: flight over residential properties (Wohngrundstücke) is only permitted with the explicit consent of the affected owner or other user, or if the aircraft is at most 0.25 kg take off mass AND is incapable of optical or acoustic recording and transmission, or from at least 100 m altitude with a legitimate purpose. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21h.html [verified]
- Since every FPV quad by definition carries a camera and a video transmitter, the 0.25 kg escape hatch in section 21h(3) number 7(b) is unavailable to FPV pilots even on a sub 250 g whoop. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21h.html [verified]
- LuftVO section 21h(3) number 6 restricts flight over nature reserves and national parks, with a carve out that expressly excludes operations "zu Zwecken des Sports oder der Freizeitgestaltung", that is, sport and recreation, so hobby flying is the use case least likely to qualify. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21h.html [verified]
- LuftVO section 21k exempts authorities and emergency response organisations flying under 25 kg from Article 12 authorisation, from the section 21h and 21i geographical zone rules, and from the direct remote identification add-on requirement. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21k.html [verified]
- United Kingdom: Article 94 of the Air Navigation Order 2016, which contained the old UK small unmanned aircraft rules, was omitted on 31 December 2020 by The Air Navigation (Amendment) Order 2020, S.I. 2020/1555, article 6, with article 8 revoking the remaining small unmanned aircraft articles. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2016/765/article/94 and https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/1555/contents/made [verified]
- The UK then carried Regulation (EU) 2019/947 into domestic law as assimilated legislation, which is why legislation.gov.uk hosts a UK specific version of 2019/947 with its own amendment history. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2019/947/contents [verified]
- The Unmanned Aircraft (Amendment) Regulations 2025, S.I. 2025/1106, brought the UK class marking regime into force on 1 January 2026: regulation 23 omitted Article 20, regulation 25 omitted Article 22, and regulations 3 and 15 renamed the UAS classes from the EU C0 to C6 naming to UK naming. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/1106/contents/made [verified]
- The same instrument inserted Article 20A into the UK version of 2019/947: "Before 1st January 2028, this Regulation applies to a UAS of an EU class as if it were a UAS of the equivalent UK class", with equivalence keyed on the number in the class name. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2019/947/article/20A [verified]
- So as of 2026 the UK has diverged from the EU on paper (its own class marks) while keeping practical equivalence until 1 January 2028, and it deleted the transitional and legacy provisions that the EU closed off two years earlier at the end of 2023. Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/1106/contents/made and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022R0425 [verified]
- S.I. 2025/1106 also amended points UAS.OPEN.020, .030, .040, .050 and .060 of Part A of the Annex, so the UK observer and VLOS wording is no longer guaranteed to be word for word identical to the EU text and must be read from the UK version. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/1106/contents/made [verified]
- Belgium: the FPS Mobility and Transport (Federale Overheidsdienst Mobiliteit en Vervoer / DGLV) is the competent authority and publishes monthly drone statistics as open data, covering valid A1/A3 and A2 remote pilot certificates, registered UAS operators, and specific category PDRA and SORA authorisations, from end 2020 onward. https://mobilit.belgium.be/nl/documenten-en-statistieken/open-data/luchtvaart/drones [verified]
- Belgium's own description of the specific category is a useful plain statement of where FPV can fall out of the open category: flights above 120 m AGL, over people or assemblies of people, beyond visual line of sight (BVLOS), or dropping items. https://mobilit.belgium.be/nl/documenten-en-statistieken/open-data/luchtvaart/drones [verified]

## RF and spectrum rules

### The standard 5.8 GHz FPV channel plan

- The five legacy analogue bands and their channel frequencies in MHz, as hardcoded in Betaflight's `vtx_table.c`, are: Boscam A 5865, 5845, 5825, 5805, 5785, 5765, 5745, 5725; Boscam B 5733, 5752, 5771, 5790, 5809, 5828, 5847, 5866; Boscam E 5705, 5685, 5665, 5645, 5885, 5905, 5925, 5945; FatShark 5740, 5760, 5780, 5800, 5820, 5840, 5860, 5880; Raceband 5658, 5695, 5732, 5769, 5806, 5843, 5880, 5917. https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/4.4-maintenance/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c [verified]
- That is 40 channel slots across 39 unique frequencies (5880 appears in both FatShark and Raceband), spanning 5645 MHz to 5945 MHz, a 300 MHz span. Source: arithmetic over the Betaflight table above [verified]

### United States

- 47 CFR 97.301(a) lists the 5 cm amateur band as 5.650 to 5.925 GHz in ITU Region 2, which covers the United States, available to a control operator holding a Technician, General, Advanced or Amateur Extra class licence. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-97/section-97.301 [verified]
- The entire 5645 to 5945 MHz FPV span therefore sits inside or immediately at the edges of the US amateur 5 cm band, and a Technician licence, the entry level US amateur licence, is sufficient. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-97/section-97.301 [verified]
- The amateur allocation is secondary in parts: 97.303(r)(1) requires amateur stations transmitting in 5.650 to 5.725 GHz not to cause harmful interference to stations authorised by other nations in the mobile except aeronautical mobile service, and 97.303(r)(2) imposes the same on 5.850 to 5.925 GHz relative to the fixed satellite service. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-97/section-97.303 [verified]
- 97.303(e) requires amateur stations receiving in the 5.725 to 5.875 GHz segment to accept interference from ISM equipment, which in practice means microwave ovens, Wi-Fi and everything else in the band. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-97/section-97.303 [verified]
- The FCC's 2020 5.9 GHz reallocation did not remove the amateur 5 cm allocation: the current eCFR text of 97.301(a) still reads 5.650 to 5.925 GHz for ITU Region 2. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-97/section-97.301 [verified]
- 47 CFR 15.247 covers unlicensed operation in 902 to 928 MHz, 2400 to 2483.5 MHz and 5725 to 5850 MHz, and 15.247(a) states that "operation under the provisions of this Section is limited to frequency hopping and digitally modulated intentional radiators". https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 [verified]
- That single sentence is the crisp legal reason an analogue FM FPV video transmitter cannot be authorised under 15.247: analogue FM video is neither frequency hopping nor digitally modulated. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 [verified]
- 47 CFR 15.201(b) requires all intentional radiators operating under Part 15 to be certified by a Telecommunication Certification Body before marketing, and 15.1(c) prohibits operation or marketing of a non compliant intentional radiator. Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.201 and https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.1 [verified]
- 47 CFR 15.1(b) states the fallback: an intentional radiator not operating in accordance with Part 15 must be licensed under section 301 of the Communications Act. For a 5.8 GHz FPV VTX the only realistic licence is the amateur one, which is exactly why the US FPV community treats a ham ticket as the compliance route. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.1 [verified]
- 15.247(b)(3) allows 1 watt peak conducted output for digitally modulated systems in all three bands, and 15.247(b)(1) allows 1 watt for frequency hopping in 5725 to 5850 MHz and for 2.4 GHz systems with at least 75 non overlapping hopping channels, 0.125 watts otherwise. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 [verified]
- 15.247(b)(2) sets 902 to 928 MHz frequency hopping at 1 watt for systems with at least 50 hopping channels and 0.25 watts for 25 to 49 channels, and 15.247(a)(1)(i) sets the dwell limit at 0.4 seconds average occupancy per frequency within a 20 second period for narrow hopping channels. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 [verified]
- 15.247(b)(4) ties those conducted limits to antennas of no more than 6 dBi directional gain, with a dB for dB power reduction above that, except for the fixed point to point exemptions in 15.247(c). https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 [verified]

### European Union

- ERC Recommendation 70-03, edition of February 2021, Annex 1 (non specific short range devices), band j, allows 5725 to 5875 MHz at 25 mW e.i.r.p. with no duty cycle requirement and no specified spectrum access requirement. This is the source of the "25 milliwatt" figure everyone quotes. https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf [verified]
- ETSI EN 300 440 V2.2.1 (2018-07) is the harmonised standard for short range devices in the 1 GHz to 40 GHz range, and its Table 1 and Annex B list 5725 MHz to 5875 MHz non specific short range at 25 mW e.i.r.p. That is the standard an FPV video transmitter would be tested against for the EU market. https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/300400_300499/300440/02.02.01_60/en_300440v020201p.pdf [verified]
- Applying the 5725 to 5875 MHz window to the Betaflight channel table: 27 of the 40 channel slots have their centre frequency inside the legal band. Source: arithmetic over ERC 70-03 Annex 1 band j and the Betaflight table [verified]
- Counting an analogue channel as occupying roughly 17 MHz, so centre plus or minus 8.5 MHz, only 24 of the 40 slots fit entirely inside 5725 to 5875 MHz. Source: same arithmetic, with the 17 MHz figure as an engineering approximation [single]
- Band E is entirely illegal in the EU: all eight of its channels (5645, 5665, 5685, 5705, 5885, 5905, 5925, 5945) fall outside 5725 to 5875 MHz. Source: arithmetic over ERC 70-03 Annex 1 band j and the Betaflight table [verified]
- Raceband loses half its channels: R1 5658 and R2 5695 are below the band, R7 5880 and R8 5917 are above it, leaving R3 to R6 usable and only R4, R5, R6 comfortably so. Source: same arithmetic [verified]
- Boscam A channel A8 at 5725 MHz sits exactly on the lower band edge, so its lower sideband falls outside the allocation, and FatShark F8 at 5880 is above the band entirely. Source: same arithmetic [verified]
- The practical consequence: a 40 channel VTX sold in the EU has 13 channel positions that cannot be legally transmitted on at all, and the ones pilots reach for first at race events, Raceband, are the worst affected. Source: same arithmetic [verified]
- ERC Recommendation 70-03 Annex 1 band i puts non specific SRD use of 2400 to 2483.5 MHz at only 10 mW e.i.r.p., while Annex 3 band b allows 100 mW e.i.r.p. for wideband data transmission with adequate spectrum sharing. https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf [verified]
- ETSI EN 300 328 V2.2.2 (2019-07) is the harmonised standard for 2.4 GHz wideband data transmission and states that "the RF output power for FHSS equipment shall be equal to or less than 20 dBm" and separately that "the RF output power for non-FHSS equipment shall be equal to or less than 20 dBm", where RF output power is defined as mean e.i.r.p. https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/300300_300399/300328/02.02.02_60/en_300328v020202p.pdf [verified]
- 20 dBm e.i.r.p. is 100 mW, so a 2.4 GHz ExpressLRS or Crossfire transmitter run at 250 mW, 500 mW or 1 W in the EU is between 4 and 10 times over the harmonised limit, and the limit is EIRP so antenna gain counts against you. https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/300300_300399/300328/02.02.02_60/en_300328v020202p.pdf [verified]
- The US 15.247 ceiling of 1 watt conducted versus the EU 100 mW EIRP is a factor of ten or more, which is the entire reason control link firmware ships with region locked power tables. Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 and https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/300300_300399/300328/02.02.02_60/en_300328v020202p.pdf [verified]
- Sub GHz in the EU is 863 to 870 MHz, not 902 to 928 MHz: ERC 70-03 Annex 1 band h1.0 gives 863 to 870 MHz at 25 mW e.r.p. with a 0.1 percent duty cycle, h1.1 gives 865 to 868 MHz at 25 mW e.r.p. with 1 percent, h1.5 gives 868 to 868.6 MHz at 25 mW e.r.p. with 1 percent or listen before talk, and h1.7 gives 869.4 to 869.65 MHz at 500 mW e.r.p. with a 10 percent duty cycle. https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf [verified]
- ERC 70-03 Annex 1 band h3 does allow 915 to 919.4 MHz at 25 mW e.r.p. with a 1 percent duty cycle, so the newer EU 915 MHz SRD window exists but at 25 mW and with a duty cycle, nothing like the US 1 watt. https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf [verified]
- Those duty cycle limits, not the power limits, are what make a continuously transmitting 50 Hz or 150 Hz control link hard to fit into the EU sub GHz SRD framework at all. https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf [verified]
- This is why ExpressLRS ships separate 868 MHz and 915 MHz firmware targets: they are different bands in different jurisdictions, not a labelling convention. Source: the band tables above plus ExpressLRS documentation [single]

### The gap between what is sold and what is legal

- The mismatch is structural, not incidental: 25 mW e.i.r.p. is the EU non specific SRD ceiling at 5.8 GHz, while VTXs advertising 400 mW, 600 mW, 1 W and above are routinely sold. https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf [verified]
- In the US the equivalent gap is legal category rather than power: an analogue VTX cannot be certified under 15.247 because that section is limited to frequency hopping and digitally modulated radiators, so operating one is only lawful under an amateur licence per 15.1(b). Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 and https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.1 [verified]
- A digital FPV system using an OFDM style waveform is in a different position from analogue: it can in principle meet the digitally modulated limb of 15.247 and be certified, which is why branded digital air units carry FCC IDs and unbranded analogue VTXs generally do not. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 [single]

## Effect on the hobby

- The single structural fact that shapes FPV everywhere: both major regimes define visual line of sight using the word "unaided", so goggles never satisfy it and a second human being is always the price of legal FPV flight. Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.31 and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- The spotter requirement is not a technicality that softened over time: it survived from the FAA's 2014 interpretation of the model aircraft rule through Part 107 in 2016 and into the EU regime in 2019, and it is written the same way on both continents. Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-107/section-107.31 and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32020R0639 [verified]
- Remote ID split the US hobby into three unequal groups: owners of factory drones that broadcast natively, owners of anything else who must buy and register a broadcast module, and clubs that can get a FRIA. Self built FPV quads are in groups two and three. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.115 [verified]
- The broadcast module path is worse for FPV than for camera drones because 89.115(a)(2)(ii) demands the pilot see the aircraft at all times, a requirement the FAA repeats in plain English on its own Remote ID page: "Pilots operating a drone with a Remote ID broadcast module must be able to see their drone at all times during flight." https://www.faa.gov/uas/getting_started/remote_id [verified]
- FRIAs cannot be applied for by individuals: 89.205 limits eligibility to FAA recognised community based organisations and educational institutions, so an unaffiliated FPV pilot has no route to the no equipment option except by joining a club. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.205 [verified]
- FRIAs expire: 48 calendar months under 89.225, with renewal due 120 days ahead, so club flying sites carry a recurring administrative obligation that did not exist under Section 336. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/part-89/section-89.225 [verified]
- In the EU the equivalent club mechanism is Article 16 of 2019/947, and it is territorially limited to the issuing member state, so a club authorisation does not travel across the internal market even though the underlying regulation does. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- The EU class mark system effectively excluded FPV rather than regulating it: no class C0 to C4 requirement set matches a 5 inch freestyle quad, and the only workable home is the "privately built" exemption in A3, which requires 150 metres from residential, commercial, industrial or recreational areas. Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0945 and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- The definition of privately built UAS explicitly excludes "UAS assembled from sets of parts placed on the market as a single ready-to-assemble kit", which puts a boxed BNF or kit quad in a materially different legal position from the same components bought loose. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- The end of the EU transitional period on 31 December 2023 was the moment the pre 2019 legacy fleet lost its softer conditions, and after 1 January 2024 the practical open category home for FPV became A3 alone. Sources: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022R0425 and https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019R0947 [verified]
- Germany layered a national restriction on top that is harsher for FPV than the EU baseline: no flight over residential property without owner consent, with the light aircraft exemption conditioned on having no camera, which no FPV aircraft satisfies. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/luftvo_2015/__21h.html [verified]
- On the radio side the hobby operates on gear that is mostly not certifiable where it is sold: 25 mW e.i.r.p. is the EU ceiling for the 5.8 GHz band while common VTX power settings are 400 mW and above, and analogue video cannot meet 47 CFR 15.247 in the US at any power. Sources: https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf and https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-15/section-15.247 [verified]
- The US has a legal path out through amateur radio: 47 CFR 97.301(a) gives Technician class and above the whole 5.650 to 5.925 GHz band, which is why the entry level ham licence became a de facto part of the American FPV pipeline. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-97/section-97.301 [verified]
- The EU has no equivalent easy path for the same hardware, since the 25 mW SRD allocation is where consumer gear must sit and the amateur route depends on national licensing rather than on a harmonised standard. https://docdb.cept.org/download/25c41779-cd6e/Rec7003e.pdf [verified]
- 13 of the 40 standard FPV channel positions cannot legally be transmitted on in the EU at all, and the affected set includes the whole of band E plus half of Raceband, which is the band race organisers reach for first. Source: arithmetic over ERC 70-03 Annex 1 band j and the Betaflight channel table [verified]
- FRIA sponsorship shows how narrow the club route really is: of 2,688 FRIAs, 1,913 are sponsored by the Academy of Model Aeronautics, 636 by educational institutions, 59 by the Flite Test Community Association, 53 by the FPV Freedom Coalition and 27 by STEM+C Inc. https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/FAA_Recognized_Identification_Areas/FeatureServer/0 [verified]
- The FPV specific CBO therefore sponsors just under 2 percent of all FRIAs in the United States, while a fixed wing and helicopter model aviation body sponsors 71 percent. Source: arithmetic over the FAA FRIA dataset [verified]
- There are exactly four FAA recognised CBOs: the Academy of Model Aeronautics, the First Person View Freedom Coalition, the Flite Test Community Association and STEM+C Inc. https://www.faa.gov/uas/recreationalfliers/faa-recognized-community-based-organizations [verified]
- The FRIA system was built in its first year and has barely grown since: 1,749 established in 2023, 687 in 2024, 182 in 2025 and 70 in 2026 to date. https://services6.arcgis.com/ssFJjBXIUyZDrSYZ/arcgis/rest/services/FAA_Recognized_Identification_Areas/FeatureServer/0 [verified]
- AMA reported roughly 2,400 clubs and 2,500 clubs and flying sites in its 2022 Annual Report, so its 1,913 FRIAs represent roughly 77 to 80 percent coverage of its own club network. https://www.modelaircraft.org/sites/default/files/AMA-2022-Annual-Report.pdf [verified]
- AMA states in its 2025 Annual Report that it has obtained a FRIA or a Remote ID Exemption for every club that applied, plus over 150 short term waivers for AMA events in 2025 and altitude authorisations to 700 to 1,200 ft AGL for over 1,000 club sites from April 2025. https://www.modelaircraft.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/2025_AMA_AnnualReport.pdf [verified]
- The concrete regulatory linkage for US FPV racing runs through the same body: MultiGP states "The only credential we require is an Academy of Model Aeronautics Membership (AMA)", and reports over 30,000 registered pilots across 500 active chapters worldwide. https://www.multigp.com/league-info/ and https://www.multigp.com/chapters/ [verified]
- MultiGP also encourages an FCC Technician class amateur licence for video transmission, which is the racing community formalising the ham radio route described in the spectrum section. https://www.multigp.com/league-info/ [verified]
- Recreational drone registration in the US is now shrinking: new recreational owner registrations fell from 6,053 per month in CY2023 to 5,810 in CY2024 to 4,782 in CY2025, and cancellations and expirations exceeded new registrations by 2,133 per month in CY2024 and 2,907 per month in CY2025. https://www.faa.gov/data_research/aviation/aerospace_forecasts/FY_2026-2046_Full_Forecast_Document_Tables.pdf [verified]
- The FAA's own conclusion: "These trends suggest that the operation of sUAS for recreational purposes may be on the decline." https://www.faa.gov/data_research/aviation/aerospace_forecasts/FY_2026-2046_Full_Forecast_Document_Tables.pdf [verified]
- The counterpoint matters as much as the point: over the same period Part 107 registrations and remote pilot certificates kept growing, so regulation coincides with a decline in the recreational segment specifically, not in drone use overall. https://www.faa.gov/data_research/aviation/aerospace_forecasts/FY_2026-2046_Full_Forecast_Document_Tables.pdf [verified]
- AMA's own diagnosis does not put regulation first: an AMA post dated 23 July 2026 states that "since 2002, paid membership has steadily declined due to headwinds from online gaming, Internet culture, the loss of traditional hobby shops, and government overreach". https://amablog.modelaircraft.org/blog/membership-360-a-plan-to-drive-growth/ [verified]
- That dating is the most important nuance available on this whole question: AMA traces its decline to 2002, thirteen years before the registration rule, and names regulation last of four causes. https://amablog.modelaircraft.org/blog/membership-360-a-plan-to-drive-growth/ [verified]
- The popular claim that the sub 250 g Tinywhoop and cinewhoop boom was driven by the registration threshold does not survive dating: Tiny Whoop states it was created in 2015, and the registration IFR took effect 21 December 2015, so the threshold cannot be its cause. Sources: https://www.tinywhoop.com/pages/about-us and https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2015-31750.json [verified]
- The 250 g line is demonstrably a design target for consumer camera drones rather than for FPV: "Under 249 g" is the first listed product highlight on DJI's Mini 4 Pro page, while a BetaFPV Pavo Pico is 71.20 g without battery, far below any threshold that could constrain its design. Sources: https://www.dji.com/mini-4-pro and https://betafpv.com/products/pavo-pico-brushless-whoop-quadcopter [verified]
- FAA UAS sighting reports peaked before Remote ID and have not returned to that level: FY2020 1,648, FY2021 2,478, FY2024 1,636, FY2025 1,931, FY2026 Q1 to Q3 1,222. https://www.faa.gov/uas/resources/public_records/uas_sightings_report [verified]
- Those are sighting reports, unverified pilot and controller observations, not collisions, and must not be converted into an incident or collision rate. https://www.faa.gov/uas/resources/public_records/uas_sightings_report [verified]
- Published FPV market size estimates are unusable: for overlapping 2024 and 2025 periods, figures range from $287 million to $2.15 billion, a spread of a factor of 7.5, with no published methodology. The honest claim is that no reliable FPV market size exists. Source: comparison of six commercial market reports [single]
- The Drone Racing League, founded 2015 and funded with $12M Series A in September 2016 and $20M Series B in 2017, is now described in the past tense, but nothing found attributes its collapse to regulation: it reads as a media business failure. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drone_Racing_League [single]
- The Remote ID rulemaking is the clearest evidence that organised hobbyist comment changes outcomes: the FAA received 53,000 comments and abandoned the internet connectivity and USS architecture entirely between NPRM and final rule. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The FAA itself framed that 53,000 figure as exceptional, contrasting it against roughly 2,600 comments EASA received on its U-Space Opinion for a comparable rulemaking. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/xml/2021/01/15/2020-28948.xml [verified]
- The direction of travel as of 2026 is toward normalising BVLOS commercially while leaving recreational FPV bound by VLOS: the Part 108 NPRM is 180 Federal Register pages aimed at commercial low altitude BVLOS and UTM, not at hobby flying. https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents/2025-14992.json [verified]

## Unknowns / could not verify

- The single biggest gap, and the most valuable thing to chase next: any documented FPV retailer closure, product withdrawal, or price increase attributable to the December 2025 FCC Covered List action. The legal mechanism is verified, the downstream retail effect is not. The same applies to DJI FPV specifically, the O3 and O4 air units, DJI Goggles and Avata.
- Whether any Brennan v. Dickson cert petition was filed and its outcome: not verified.
- TRUST completion totals, the TRUST launch date, and the number of approved test administrators: no FAA published figures located.
- The number of accepted Remote ID declarations of compliance, standard and broadcast module: uasdoc.faa.gov is a JavaScript application whose data endpoint was not reachable.
- Whether any Open Source flight controller firmware Remote ID implementation appears on the FAA declaration of compliance list: not verified.
- Whether the 2,688 FRIA records equal 2,688 distinct physical sites: treat as a record count.
- Exact AMA membership counts for any year. AMA marketing copy claims "more than 175,000" (2015), "more than 195,000" (2016 and 2018), "nearly 200,000" (2020) and "165,000" (2026), but these are boilerplate, not audited figures, and they rise across a period AMA itself describes as decline. Do not build an argument on the 195,000 to 165,000 delta. The 990 revenue series is the better proxy.
- 2016, 2017 and 2018 FAA registration totals: no archived by_the_numbers snapshot exists before August 2019, and the "300,000 in 30 days" and "one million registered" milestone claims were not traced to a primary FAA source.
- FY2022 and FY2023 total drone sighting counts: those quarters were published as HTML rather than spreadsheets and could not be totalled.
- CBP or UFLPA import detentions of DJI or FPV products, and the current status of the Countering CCP Drones Act (H.R. 2864) in the 119th Congress: not investigated.
- Cinewhoop weights above 250 g: the Pavo Pico at 71.2 g is verified, but weights for larger 2.5 inch and 3 inch ducted builds were not extracted, so the claim that many cinewhoops exceed 250 g is plausible but unverified.
- Whether AMA claimed public credit for specific Remote ID final rule changes, and campaign level comment totals for Pilot Institute, Drone U or the FPV Freedom Coalition: the final rule preamble records 90 FPVFC members on one form letter and 33 on a "Traditional Hobbyist Form Letter Campaign", but no campaign totals.
- Whether any FAI World Drone Racing Championship edition was moved or cancelled for regulatory reasons: not investigated.
- Belgium's national implementing Royal Decree for 2019/947, its date, the pre 2019 Royal Decree of 2016 and its class 1a/1b/2 structure, the Belgian minimum remote pilot age, and whether Belgium issued Article 16 model club authorisations: mobilit.belgium.be drone policy pages returned HTTP 404 to automated fetching, so only the open data page and its statistics were verified.
