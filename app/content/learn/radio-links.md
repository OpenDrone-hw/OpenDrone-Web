# RC radio link history research

Status: complete. Research done 2026-08-12.
Tagging: [verified] = two independent sources or one primary source. [single] = one secondary source only.
Note on method: GitHub REST API was queried directly for repo creation dates, commit dates, release dates and contributor counts, so those bullets are primary-source.

## Link generations

### Analog era: 27, 35, 40, 72 and 75 MHz

- Source hygiene note: several claims below were reached through search-result snippets rather than a retrievable primary page, because this session's search budget was spent and Google, Bing and Brave return JavaScript shells to non-browser clients. Those are tagged [single] and flagged inline.
- The first US RC allocation for home-built equipment was 27.255 MHz, authorised under FCC Docket 10086 on 1952-03-24, requiring crystal control, under 5 watts, and no operator exam (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- Five further 27 MHz RC channels were granted on 1958-09-11: 26.995, 27.045, 27.095, 27.145 and 27.195 MHz, after a 1956 to 1958 campaign led by communications attorney Ed Ansell (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- Those five channels at 50 kHz spacing were physically interleaved among 22 Citizens Band voice channels spaced 10 kHz apart, the structural reason 27 MHz RC was chronically jammed (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- The CB overlap forced modellers off wideband super-regenerative receivers onto narrow-band superhet designs (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- The six 27 MHz channels were identified by coloured flags on the transmitter aerial: brown 26.995, red 27.045, orange 27.095, yellow 27.145, green 27.195, blue 27.255 (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/fcc-approves-ama-petition-new-rc-frequencies, plus BMFA Handbook via search snippet) [single]
- Documented 27 MHz shootdown, 1958 Chicago Nationals: RC events were halted during afternoon rush hour because a 200 watt traffic control transmitter was operating on 27.255 MHz (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- Documented injury case in California: a CB operator illegally amplifying to 40 watts on an RC channel caused an out of control aircraft to crash into a spectator, leading to FCC prosecution (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- Other named 27 MHz interference sources of the era were medical diathermy equipment and illegally modified CB sets (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- The US 72 MHz band was won in 1963 to 1966: the FCC approved five 72 MHz channels in June 1966 on a 3 to 2 vote, with dissenting Commissioners describing radio control as "essentially trivial" (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- The AMA Frequency Committee for that fight was chaired by Ed Lorenz, with attorney Jerry Courtney representing modellers (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/battle-rc-frequencies-1) [single]
- The big reallocation came from an FCC NPRM approved 1982-04-01 and a Report and Order approved 1982-11-04 on a 6 to 0 vote (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/fcc-approves-ama-petition-new-rc-frequencies, Model Aviation February 1983) [single]
- That order took effect 1983-01-01 and created the split that defined the rest of the analog era: 72 MHz channels 11 through 60 for model aircraft only, 75 MHz channels 61 through 90 for surface models (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/fcc-approves-ama-petition-new-rc-frequencies, http://library.modelaviation.com/article/newcomers-36) [verified]
- The 1982 order deliberately let old and new 72 MHz frequencies coexist for five years, so only alternate channels were usable at first, and only 13 aircraft channels were released for immediate phase-in (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/fcc-approves-ama-petition-new-rc-frequencies) [single]
- The full 50 channel plan was gated on AMA action, not FCC action: "As of January 1, 1991, the AMA will sanction use of all channels (RC11 through RC60) in the 72 MHz band for aircraft use only" (George M. Myers, Model Aviation February 1987, http://library.modelaviation.com/article/radio-technique-119, corroborated at http://library.modelaviation.com/article/radio-technique-142 and https://www.modelaviation.com/is-72-mhz-dead) [verified]
- The 1991 change was narrowbanding, not new spectrum: the FCC granted no extra bandwidth and instead permitted narrow-band equipment so more channels fitted in the same 1 MHz (https://www.modelaviation.com/is-72-mhz-dead) [single]
- Post-1991 the plan was 50 channels at 20 kHz spacing, channel 11 at 72.010 MHz through channel 60 at 72.990 MHz (https://www.modelaviation.com/is-72-mhz-dead, http://aviationfunfly.com/ModelRadio.htm) [verified]
- Myers warned in 1987 that 20 kHz spacing would create adjacent-channel interference above RC38 because converted wideband AM sets and pre-1986 gear would not be selective enough (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/radio-technique-119) [single]
- The receiver-side failure mode was specific: single conversion 455 kHz IF receivers have a local oscillator that almost coincides with the carrier of any RC transmitter 23 channels away, so an SC455 could be captured by a transmitter nowhere near its own channel (George Myers, Model Aviation April 1989, http://library.modelaviation.com/article/radio-technique-142) [single]
- Myers concluded in 1989 that wideband SC455 receivers "will not be of much use in the 72 MHz Aircraft-Only band after 1991", which is why dual conversion became the de facto requirement (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/radio-technique-142) [single]
- The FCC still lists 50 channels in 72.0 to 73.0 MHz as available for model aircraft, so the band was never withdrawn, it was abandoned commercially (https://www.fcc.gov/wireless/bureau-divisions/mobility-division/radio-control-radio-service-rcrs) [single]
- 50 to 54 MHz RC operation in the US required a licensed amateur, and 53 MHz frequencies remained available after the 1983 replan (http://library.modelaviation.com/article/fcc-approves-ama-petition-new-rc-frequencies) [single]
- In Europe the 35 MHz band is aircraft-only by rule, and must not be used for surface vehicles (BMFA Handbook via search snippet) [single]
- UK 35 MHz channel numbering ran 55 to 90, with an expansion adding channels 55 to 59 and 81 to 90 (search snippet, BMFA-derived) [single]
- UK identification convention differed by spacing: a coloured ribbon on the aerial for 20 kHz spacing, a white flag with the channel number in black for 10 kHz spacing (BMFA Handbook via search snippet) [single]
- The European 40 MHz band was surface-only, 34 channels, marked with green flags carrying white channel numbers (search snippet) [single]
- Field etiquette was enforced by a physical frequency control board plus transmitter impound, with the standard club rule that transmitters may not leave the impound unless the frequency pin is attached (multiple independent club rulebooks via search snippets) [single]
- The purpose was explicitly anti-shootdown: pilots tracked who else at the field was on their frequency so they did not shoot another pilot's aircraft down (club rulebooks via search snippets) [single]
- Clubs universally exempt 2.4 GHz users from the frequency board, the cleanest evidence of what 2.4 GHz actually eliminated (club rulebooks via search snippets) [single]
- The 72 MHz digital proportional lineage ran AM, then FM, then PCM, in that order (https://www.modelaviation.com/is-72-mhz-dead) [single]

### PPM and PCM encoding

- The standard RC servo pulse is 1 ms to 2 ms with 1.5 ms neutral, repeated at roughly 20 ms, and position is set by pulse width alone, not duty cycle (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servo_control) [single]
- The 20 ms servo convention is a fossil of the radio frame: the PPM period was around 22.5 ms and conversion to servo PWM was trivial, the servo pulse width being the time position of that channel's PPM pulse (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servo_control) [single]
- Measured on real hardware, a Futaba PPM frame is 22.5 ms and the same brand's PCM frame is 14.25 ms, so PCM was also lower latency, not only more robust (http://mmi.tudelft.nl/~wouter/publications/pasman03i.pdf) [single]
- The 22.5 ms frame is sized for 8 channels, with roughly 2 ms added per extra channel beyond 8, which is why 9 or more channel PPM stretches the frame (http://openrcforums.com/forum/viewtopic.php?t=10889, http://openrcforums.com/forum/viewtopic.php?t=4134) [verified]
- PPM shift polarity was brand-specific and not fixable by rewiring: positive shift for JR, Sanwa/Airtronics and KO, negative shift for Futaba and Hitec, and it mattered only on 27 and 72 MHz, not on 35/36/40 MHz (search snippets) [single]
- Futaba's PCM entry point was the FP-8SGAP, developed for FAI F3A aerobatics, with a microprocessor in both transmitter and receiver (search snippet) [single]
- Futaba's PCM line ran PCM512 first, in the FP-8SGA-P and FP-8SSH-P, and the first PCM1024 set was the FP-9VAP (RCGroups thread via search snippet) [single]
- JR's PCM10 appeared around 1988 and was unchanged until the "S" version in late 1992 or early 1993 (https://www.modelairplanenews.com/old-school-jr-10sx-ii-keeping-my-favorite-radio-alive/) [single]
- The functional gain of PCM over PPM was error detection leading to failsafe and hold: the receiver can distinguish a corrupted frame from a valid one, which an analog PPM receiver cannot do because every pulse edge is nominally valid (https://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?91228-Futaba-PCM-decoding) [single]
- PCM was proprietary per brand because the bit-level encoding was never standardised, so transmitter and receiver had to match brand and often generation (https://www.modelaviation.com/is-72-mhz-dead, https://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?91228-Futaba-PCM-decoding) [verified]

### The FCC rule change that enabled 2.4 GHz RC

- The enabling rulemaking was the Second Report and Order in ET Docket 99-231, FCC 02-151, adopted 2002-05-16, released 2002-05-30, published at 67 FR 42730 on 2002-06-25, effective 2002-07-25 (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2002/06/25/02-15951/spread-spectrum-devices, https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-02-151A1.pdf) [verified]
- That order removed the regulatory distinction between DSSS and other digital modulation, replacing references to "direct sequence spread spectrum" with "digital modulation" and permitting all digitally modulated systems under 15.247 (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2002/06/25/02-15951/spread-spectrum-devices) [verified]
- It eliminated the 10 dB minimum processing gain requirement for direct sequence systems, removing a certification obstacle for low-data-rate proprietary links (same source) [verified]
- It created the low-hop-count option most RC systems use: frequency hopping systems may use as few as fifteen hopping channels with bandwidths up to 5 MHz and no minimum band occupancy, provided output power is reduced to 125 mW (same source) [verified]
- The causal timing is the key fact: the order took effect 2002-07-25 and Spektrum unveiled DSM in October 2004, so first-generation RC 2.4 GHz products land immediately downstream of it (Federal Register; https://my.spektrumrc.com/About/) [verified]

### Spektrum: DSM, DSM2, DSMX

- Horizon Hobby unveiled Spektrum 2.4 GHz Digital Spectrum Modulation at the iHobby Expo in Rosemont, Illinois in October 2004 (https://my.spektrumrc.com/About/) [verified]
- Independent corroboration: spektrumrc.com's first Internet Archive capture is 2004-10-18 (http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=www.spektrumrc.com) [verified]
- The October 2004 site defined the product as the 72 MHz pain points inverted: "It requires no crystals and completely frees you from ever having to monitor frequency usage again" (http://web.archive.org/web/20041018002205/http://www.spektrumrc.com/) [verified]
- The same page lists DSM basics: no frequency monitoring, immunity to common RF interference, worldwide 2.4 GHz ISM acceptance, a purely digital RF link, a return data path, built-in failsafe, 4096 step resolution, no crystals (same archived page) [verified]
- DSM launched as a module retrofit, not a radio: it plugged into existing 3-channel module-based transmitters such as the JR R-1 and Airtronics M-8 (same archived page) [verified]
- A Spektrum article dated 2004-10-20 gives the original DSM link budget: 2.400 to 2.4835 GHz, 79 channels, 4096 bit resolution, 5.6 ms response time, 3000 ft range, over 4.2 billion GUID codes (https://my.spektrumrc.com/Articles/Article.aspx?ArticleID=1423) [verified]
- Spektrum claimed an 18 dB processing gain improvement from its DSSS choice (same article) [single]
- Original DSM was explicitly optimised for surface first: the DSSS implementation "has been optimized for RC car use" (same article) [verified]
- Early public validation was on the car side, at the US Indoor Championships in Cleveland and the IFMAR On-Road Electric World Championships in Kissimmee, with drivers Mark Pavidis, Tyree Phillips and Brian Kinwald (https://my.spektrumrc.com/About/) [verified]
- The first all-in-one Spektrum transmitter was the DX3, a 3-channel surface radio, debuting May 2005 at the RCX convention in Los Angeles with an SR3000 receiver (https://my.spektrumrc.com/About/) [verified]
- Surface therefore preceded air for Spektrum, in both technology framing and shipping product (https://my.spektrumrc.com/About/, https://www.modelaircraft.org/sites/default/files/BeardPaul.pdf) [verified]
- The DX6 was the first Spektrum air radio, a park flyer rather than full range, released October 2005 (AMA History Project biography of Paul Beard; https://forum.flitetest.com/index.php?threads/the-history-of-spektrum-radios.53508/) [verified]
- Andy Kunz, a Spektrum programmer, confirms in a 2018-04-30 post: "DX6 (G1) - first 2.4gHz air radio, for park flying. Supported only DSM protocol" (https://forum.flitetest.com/index.php?threads/the-history-of-spektrum-radios.53508/) [single]
- The DX6 used the Cypress CYWUSB6935 WirelessUSB transceiver, with 49 Gold spreading codes across 78 channels of 1 MHz (https://www.airplanesandrockets.com/electronics/SpektrumDX6/spektrum-dx6-spread-spectrum-radio-system.htm) [single]
- DSM2 arrived with the DX7 in October 2006, launched at the iHobby Expo in Chicago, marketed as the first full range 2.4 GHz spread spectrum radio system for RC aircraft (AMA Beard biography; Spektrum DX7 manual) [verified]
- The technical change in DSM2 was redundancy in frequency, not hopping: original DSM used a single channel, DSM2 uses DSSS on two channels selected at start up (https://sites.google.com/site/txwingsrc/links/rc-protocols, http://chathamaeronauts.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/2017-01-31-DSM-DSM2-DSMX.pdf) [verified]
- DSM2 added ModelMatch, tying a receiver to a specific model memory so the transmitter relinks without rebinding (https://my.spektrumrc.com/) [single]
- DSMX was announced 2011-02-03 (http://modelflight.blogspot.com/2011/02/introducing-spektrum-dsmx-true.html, reproducing the Horizon Hobby release) [single]
- DSMX kept DSM2's wideband DSSS and added frequency agility, each transmitter computing its own hop pattern from its GUID and using 23 channels of the band, plus MultiLink dual-path redundancy (same source) [single]
- Spektrum's validation claim for DSMX was 100 systems run simultaneously with no link loss, latency increase or control degradation (same source) [single]
- Spektrum markets DSMX as "the world's first wideband, frequency-agile 2.4GHz signal protocol", a narrower claim than first frequency hopping RC system and worth reading as such (https://my.spektrumrc.com/Technology/DSMX.aspx) [single]
- DSM2 and DSMX are cross-compatible, with full DSMX benefit only when both ends are DSMX (Horizon Hobby release, 2011) [single]
- Paul Beard invented DSM and is called "the Father of Spread Spectrum" in the AMA History Project, and is an AMA Model Aviation Hall of Fame inductee (https://www.modelaircraft.org/sites/default/files/BeardPaul.pdf) [verified]
- Beard's background explains the chip choice: British Telecom from 1978, US in 1989 at VMX Inc, founded Alation Systems in 1998, and "invented, launched, and sold the WirelessUSB product line" through it (same AMA biography) [verified]
- Beard began looking for an interference-free RC link in 2001 and became vice president of engineering at Horizon Hobby in 2004 (same AMA biography) [verified]
- Spektrum's own 2004 launch article describes Beard as a leading spread spectrum engineer who designed DSM after "development for over four years", consistent with a 2001 start (https://my.spektrumrc.com/Articles/Article.aspx?ArticleID=1423) [verified]
- Charles Anderson, president of the International Radio Controlled Helicopter Association, called Beard's work "the single greatest advancement in 30 years" in the AMA nomination (AMA biography) [verified]

### Futaba FASST and successors

- FASST stands for Futaba Advanced Spread Spectrum Technology, described by Futaba as its first 2.4 GHz system, using an industrial-class spread spectrum processing IC (https://futabausa.com/futaba-rc-tech/air-radio-communication-systems/) [verified]
- FASST is a hybrid spread spectrum scheme, not pure FHSS or pure DSSS, and it is one-way with no telemetry; some receivers support a 7 ms high speed refresh (same source) [verified]
- Archival evidence bounds the US launch: futaba-rc.com homepage captures from 2006-01-04 and 2006-05-04 contain zero occurrences of "FASST" or "2.4", while FASST logo assets are present by 2007-02-24 (http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=futaba-rc.com) [verified]
- Futaba's FASST therefore reached the US market roughly two years after Spektrum DSM and after DSM2, so Futaba was a fast follower rather than a co-pioneer (Wayback CDX; https://my.spektrumrc.com/About/) [verified]
- Early FASST hardware includes the R603FS 3-channel receiver listed as 2006, the 6EX-2.4GHz, and the T7C-2.4G with R617FS receiver (https://futabausa.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/6ex-2_4ghz-manual.pdf) [single]
- FASSTest is the hybrid DSSS plus FHSS successor with telemetry, offering 26 channels (24 proportional plus 2 toggle) or 18 channels, and a 12-channel mode running more than twice the speed of normal mode (https://futabausa.com/futaba-rc-tech/air-radio-communication-systems/) [verified]
- FASSTest adds the "Pre-Vision Diversity System", where the receiver predicts in advance which of its two antennas will be able to receive (same source) [verified]
- S-FHSS is the cheap one-way protocol capped at 8 channels with no telemetry; T-FHSS AIR succeeds it, adds telemetry and has FASSTest-comparable range but no 12-channel high speed mode (same source) [verified]
- Measured servo refresh differs materially between the budget protocols: S-FHSS 147 Hz (6.8 ms), T-FHSS 333 Hz (3.0 ms) (rctech.net forum) [single]
- A European regulatory change effective 2015-01-01 is the reason newer Futaba radios such as the T26SZ dropped original FASST support (https://matttakharrc.uk/blog/why-the-futaba-t26sz-doesnt-support-the-original-fasst-protocol) [single]

### Other early 2.4 GHz systems

- The Nomadio Sensor, a 2.4 GHz DSSS surface radio, was discussed on RC Universe forums as early as 2004-04-27, which places it at or before Spektrum's October 2004 unveiling (rcuniverse.com threads via search snippet) [single]
- At least one RC source credits Nomadio, not Spektrum, with the first 2.4 GHz RC system, while noting Spektrum is where the industry-wide switch began (search snippet) [single]
- Nomadio marketed the Sensor as the first two-way digital radio control system for hobbyists, with battery voltage, temperature and RPM feedback plus USB firmware upgrades, so telemetry was its headline (search snippet) [single]
- nomadio.com has no Internet Archive capture before 2007-02-09, so the 2004 dating rests on forum threads rather than the company's own site (http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=nomadio.com) [verified]
- Xtreme Power Systems of Lake Havasu City sold XtremeLink 2.4 GHz modules and 8-channel receivers for Futaba, JR, Hitec and Graupner tray radios; the principal is Jim Drew (https://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?566018-XtremeLink-2-4GHz-for-Futaba-JR-and-Hitec-radios, https://www.xtremepowersystems.net) [single]
- Correction worth carrying: Jim Bourke, often conflated here, is the founder of RCGroups.com, not of Xtreme Power Systems (same sources) [single]
- Hitec's AFHSS entry was the Aurora 9 with the Spectra 2.4 module and Optima receivers; the hitecrcd.com asset announcing it carries a cache-buster timestamp of 1233807468, which is 2009-02-05, with first capture 2009-07-03 (http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=hitecrcd.com) [verified]
- Hitec's Spectra 2.4 module works only with Hitec AFHSS receivers, so it was a closed retrofit path like everyone else's (https://www.hitecrcd.com/products/aircraft-radios-receivers-and-accesories/2-4ghz/spectra24j.html) [single]
- JR's DMSS is dated by JR's own blog post at jrpropo.co.jp/blog/2011/01/dmss.html with English DMSS image assets filed under 2010/10, so the announcement window is October 2010 to January 2011; the JR XG8 followed mid 2011 (http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=jrpropo.co.jp) [verified]
- DMSS, Dual Modulation Spectrum System, combines DSSS and FHSS into a wideband transmission (https://www.jrpropo.co.jp/eng/) [single]
- Airtronics is the US brand of Sanwa and its 2.4 GHz protocols ran FHSS-1, FHSS-2 then FHSS-3, with the SD-10G 10-channel aircraft radio on FHSS-3 (manualslib, flyrc.com) [single]
- Multiplex fielded M-LINK with its own MSB-based telemetry, deliberately incompatible with Graupner HoTT and Jeti Duplex (search snippet) [single]

### FlySky

- The company is FLYSKY Model Ltd., with branches in Shenzhen and Dongguan, founded in 2006 (https://www.flysky-cn.com/about) [verified]
- AFHDS stands for Automatic Frequency Hopping Digital System, and AFHDS 2A is "Automatic Frequency Hopping Digital System Second Generation" (FlySky FS-i6X manual) [verified]
- AFHDS 2A runs on the A7105 transceiver with FEC and CRC enabled, a 4 byte ID and 2 byte preamble (https://fareham.org.uk/rw3-afhds2a.shtml, reverse-engineered) [single]
- AFHDS 2A picks 16 hop channels at random during binding out of 160 possible 500 kHz channels spanning 2400 to 2483 MHz (same source) [single]
- AFHDS 2A frames go out every 3.85 ms, about 260 Hz, with roughly 1.5 ms over-air time per 37 byte packet carrying 14 channels as 16-bit microsecond values (same source) [single]
- The receiver initially syncs at half rate, 7.7 ms, then matches the 3.85 ms cadence, and the transmitter listens one channel lower for telemetry (same source) [single]
- Original AFHDS by contrast used 8 channels, a 1.544 ms frame at about 650 Hz, a preset rather than random hop sequence, no FEC and no return link (same source) [single]
- In the MULTI-Module implementation AFHDS 2A supports 14 channels natively, extended sub-protocols giving CH15, CH16 and LQI, up to 64 bound receivers, and telemetry with receiver firmware 1.6 or later (https://www.multi-module.org/using-the-module/protocol-details/flysky-afhds2a) [single]
- The FlySky FS-TH9X is the same hardware sold as the Turnigy 9X, Eurgle 9X and iMax 9X, as stated in the th9x project description (https://github.com/thus1/th9x) [verified]
- The Turnigy 9X sold through HobbyKing at USD 54.99 to 60 in 2010, against Spektrum and Futaba computer radios costing several times that, which is the entire reason it mattered (https://www.rcuniverse.com/forum/beginners-85/10191619-turnigy-9x-2-4ghz-9-channel-radio.html, https://oscarliang.com/turnigy-9x-review-9-channel-rc-transmitter/) [verified]
- The Turnigy 9X Version 2, reviewed 2010-06-22, replaced the old single-frequency non-agile DSSS system with a FlySky frequency-hopping module and receiver, dropping the satellite receiver (https://rcmodelreviews.com/turnigy9xv2review.shtml) [single]
- The FS-i6X operates 2.408 to 2.475 GHz with a stated 500 to 1500 m range and supports 10 channels "through DIY upgrades", which is the firmware-hack culture that grew around it (https://www.flysky-cn.com/fsi6x, https://github.com/SpawnCampGames/flysky-i6x) [verified]

### Pre-Crossfire commercial UHF

- ImmersionRC EzUHF existed no later than December 2011: the vendor's own firmware and driver instruction PDF is named `ezuhf_firmware_and_driver_28_12_2011.pdf` and is archived from immersionrc.com (https://web.archive.org/web/20121019065023/http://immersionrc.com/downloads/ezuhf_firmware_and_driver_28_12_2011.pdf) [verified]
- The EzUHF product line was TX plus 4ch, 8ch Lite and 8ch Diversity receivers, with a high power mode on the TX and a firmware file literally named `EzUHFFirmware_Tx500.fw`, indicating the 500 mW transmitter variant (EzUHF firmware upgrade PDF, 2011; immersionrc.com product URLs archived from 2015) [verified]
- EzUHF firmware was upgraded over USB with Windows XP/Vista/7 and "ImmersionRC tools", which is the era-typical closed toolchain that open projects later displaced (EzUHF firmware upgrade PDF, 2011) [single]
- Dragon Link's V2 user guide was written by Chris Seto and is dated "R5 8/24/2012", published as `DragonLinkV2UserGuide29Oct2012.pdf`, so Dragon Link V2 was shipping in 2012 (https://web.archive.org/web/2013/http://dragonlinkrc.com/sites/default/files/DragonLinkV2UserGuide29Oct2012.pdf) [verified]
- dragonlinkrc.com first appears in the Wayback Machine on 2012-09-21 (Wayback CDX for dragonlinkrc.com) [verified]
- Dragon Link took PPM from the host radio's trainer port: the quick start tells the user to "set your RC transmitter to output in PPM mode or activate your trainer port", which is exactly how every UHF module of the era attached to a 35/72 MHz or 2.4 GHz handset (Dragon Link V2 user guide, 2012) [verified]
- Dragon Link supported a head tracker input on the same PPM connection, a feature list item that shows UHF modules were built for FPV, not for park flyers (Dragon Link V2 user guide, 2012) [verified]

### TBS Crossfire (900 MHz LoRa)

### ExpressLRS packet rates and sensitivity (from firmware source)

- Definition from the project glossary: `FLRC` is "Fast Long Range Communication", the F-mode, combining demodulation, forward error correction and interleaving for high data rate, lower latency and comparable or shorter range than LoRa (https://github.com/ExpressLRS/Docs/blob/master/docs/info/glossary.md) [verified]
- Definition from the project glossary: `DVDA` is "Deja Vu Diversity Aid", the D-mode, which sends the same packet multiple times consecutively, 2 times for D-250 and 4 for D-500, increasing LQ at the cost of range and latency (https://github.com/ExpressLRS/Docs/blob/master/docs/info/glossary.md) [verified]
- Note: the glossary's "2 times for D-250 and 4 for D-500" is inconsistent with the firmware source, where `RATE_FLRC_2G4_250HZ_DVDA` has 4 OTA packets and `RATE_FLRC_2G4_500HZ_DVDA` has 2, both at a 1000 us interval. The firmware is authoritative, so the docs glossary has the two rates swapped (https://github.com/ExpressLRS/ExpressLRS/blob/master/src/src/common.cpp) [verified]
- SX1280 (2.4 GHz) rate table in firmware, in order: FLRC 1000 Hz, FLRC 500 Hz, FLRC 500 Hz DVDA, FLRC 250 Hz DVDA, LoRa 500 Hz, LoRa 333 Hz Full, LoRa 250 Hz, LoRa 150 Hz, LoRa 100 Hz Full, LoRa 50 Hz (https://github.com/ExpressLRS/ExpressLRS/blob/master/src/src/common.cpp) [verified]
- SX1280 receiver sensitivity figures used by the firmware, in dBm: FLRC 1000 Hz -104, FLRC 500 Hz -104, D500 -104, D250 -104, LoRa 500 Hz -105, LoRa 333 Hz Full -105, LoRa 250 Hz -108, LoRa 150 Hz -112, LoRa 100 Hz Full -112, LoRa 50 Hz -115 (`ExpressLRS_AirRateRFperf` for `RADIO_SX128X`, https://github.com/ExpressLRS/ExpressLRS/blob/master/src/src/common.cpp) [verified]
- SX127x (900 MHz) sensitivity figures in firmware, in dBm: 200 Hz -112, 100 Hz Full -112, 100 Hz -117, 50 Hz -120, 25 Hz -123, 50 Hz DVDA -112 (`ExpressLRS_AirRateRFperf` for `RADIO_SX127X`, same file) [verified]
- The link budget cost of speed is therefore about 11 dB from 2.4 GHz 50 Hz LoRa (-115 dBm) to 2.4 GHz FLRC 1000 Hz (-104 dBm), and 900 MHz 25 Hz (-123 dBm) sits a further 8 dB below the best 2.4 GHz mode (derived from firmware tables above) [verified]
- 900 MHz LoRa modes use SF9 down to SF6 at 500 kHz bandwidth; 2.4 GHz LoRa modes use SF8 down to SF5 at 800 kHz bandwidth, which is why 2.4 GHz can reach 500 Hz and 900 MHz cannot (firmware `ExpressLRS_AirRateConfig`, same file) [verified]
- Newer LR1121 targets add GFSK modes: `RATE_FSK_900_1000HZ_8CH` and `RATE_FSK_2G4_1000HZ` at 300 kbit/s GFSK, giving 1000 Hz on 900 MHz hardware for the first time, at only -104 dBm sensitivity (firmware `RADIO_LR1121` block, same file) [verified]
- ExpressLRS telemetry is slot-based: at 500 Hz with a 1:2 telemetry ratio the link carries 250 telemetry packets per second, about 5000 bps, or 9922 bps with burst mode; at 50 Hz with 1:128 it is 0.4 packets per second, about 8 bps (https://github.com/ExpressLRS/Docs/blob/master/docs/info/telem-bandwidth.md) [verified]
- Telemetry burst mode was added in ExpressLRS PR #472 and targets a minimum LINK packet every 512 ms, roughly 2 per second (https://github.com/ExpressLRS/Docs/blob/master/docs/info/telem-bandwidth.md) [verified]

## Protocols

- SBUS runs at 100000 baud, 8E2, with a 25 byte frame; Betaflight's driver defines `SBUS_BAUDRATE 100000` and `SBUS_TIME_NEEDED_PER_BYTE` as roughly 120 us per byte at 100k baud 8E2 (https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/sbus.c) [verified]
- Real measured SBUS frame timing differs by brand: Betaflight source records FrSky X8R at 6 ms between frames and 3 ms to send a frame, Futaba R6208SB/R6303SB at 11 ms between frames and 3 ms to send. So "SBUS is 9 ms" is wrong, it is receiver dependent (https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/sbus.c) [verified]
- Fast SBUS at 200000 baud with a 6 ms refresh is supported in Betaflight as `SBUS_FAST_BAUDRATE` (same file) [verified]
- IBUS (FlySky) runs at 115200 baud, non-inverted, with a 32 byte frame, 14 servo slots and up to 18 channels in Betaflight's implementation (https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/ibus.c) [verified]
- CRSF's canonical baud rate is 420000, with 416666 used on some targets whose UART clock cannot divide to 420000 exactly (`CRSF_BAUDRATE`, https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/crsf_protocol.h) [verified]
- CRSF uses 1 stop bit, no parity, and a bidirectional (MODE_RXTX) port, in contrast to SBUS's inverted 8E2, which is the concrete reason CRSF ended the inverter-mod era (`CRSF_PORT_OPTIONS`, `CRSF_PORT_MODE`, https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/crsf.h) [verified]
- CRSF carries 16 channels; CRSFv3 raised this to 24 via subset frames with selectable 10, 11, 12 or 13 bit channel resolution (`CRSF_MAX_CHANNEL 16`, `CRSFV3_MAX_CHANNEL 24`, `CRSF_SUBSET_RC_RES_*`, https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/crsf.h) [verified]
- CRSF frames are at most 64 bytes including a 2 byte header (`CRSF_FRAME_SIZE_MAX = 64`, crsf_protocol.h) [verified]
- CRSF's link statistics carry RSSI in dBm over the range -130 to 0 and SNR over -30 to +20 dB, which is why FPV OSDs can show real dBm rather than a scaled 0 to 100 RSSI (`CRSF_RSSI_MIN`, `CRSF_SNR_MIN`, crsf.h) [verified]
- CRSF entered the Open Source FC world on 2016-10-05 with Cleanflight commit 890eab20 "First cut of CRSF RX and telemetry code" by Martin Budden (GitHub handle `martinbudden`) (GitHub commit search, repo cleanflight/cleanflight) [verified]
- CRSF was made bidirectional in Cleanflight on 2016-11-16 ("Changed CRSF RX to be bidirectional") and merged into Betaflight via PR #1504 on 2016-11-20, so Crossfire telemetry support in Betaflight dates from late 2016 (GitHub commit search, cleanflight and betaflight repos) [verified]
- FrSky F.Port support landed in Betaflight on 2017-09-12, authored by `mikeller` ("Added support for the FrSky FPort protocol") (GitHub API `repos/betaflight/betaflight/commits?path=src/main/rx/fport.c`) [verified]
- Spektrum SRXL2 support landed in Betaflight on 2019-08-06 by `DieHertz`, with the commit noting "Currently only SPM4650 released", dating the SRXL2 receiver launch to 2019 (GitHub API commits for `src/main/rx/srxl2.c`) [verified]
- SRXL2 negotiates baud: it starts at 115200 and switches to 400000 after handshake (`SRXL2_PORT_BAUDRATE_DEFAULT 115200`, `SRXL2_PORT_BAUDRATE_HIGH 400000`, https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/srxl2.c) [verified]
- GHST (ImmersionRC Ghost) support landed in Betaflight on 2020-09-26, initial commit "Initial install of GHST driver" by `tonycake`, dating Ghost's FC integration to Q3 2020 (GitHub API commits for `src/main/rx/ghst.c`) [verified]
- GHST runs the RX link at 420000 baud, same as CRSF, with a TX-direction fast mode at 400000 and slow mode at 115200, and a compact 14 byte frame with a 10 byte payload (https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/ghst_protocol.h) [verified]
- GHST uses a half duplex bidirectional push-pull single wire port (`SERIAL_BIDIR | SERIAL_BIDIR_PP`, https://github.com/betaflight/betaflight/blob/master/src/main/rx/ghst.c) [verified]
- ExpressLRS's own glossary states LQ (percentage of expected packets received) is "our preferred method of measuring the quality of the control link", over RSSI, which it calls an arbitrary scaled value (https://github.com/ExpressLRS/Docs/blob/master/docs/info/glossary.md) [verified]

## RF fundamentals and regulation

- Free space path loss at 1 km is 91.7 dB at 915 MHz, 91.2 dB at 868 MHz and 100.2 dB at 2.44 GHz, so 2.4 GHz starts 8.5 to 9.0 dB behind 900 MHz before any obstruction is considered (computed from FSPL = 20log10(d) + 20log10(f) - 147.55 dB with d in metres and f in Hz) [verified]
- That 8.5 dB is worth a factor of 2.67 in range at equal transmit power, receiver sensitivity and antenna gain, which is exactly the ratio of the two carrier frequencies (derived from the same formula) [verified]
- The old bands were far better still on pure path loss: FSPL at 1 km is 63.3 dB at 35 MHz and 69.6 dB at 72 MHz, roughly 31 to 37 dB better than 2.4 GHz, which is why a 35 MHz set with a 500 mW-class transmitter and a whip never needed line of sight discipline (computed, same formula) [verified]
- The antenna length penalty runs the other way: a quarter wave is 2141 mm at 35 MHz, 1041 mm at 72 MHz, 86 mm at 868 MHz, 82 mm at 915 MHz and 31 mm at 2.44 GHz. That is the real reason 2.4 GHz took over consumer RC, receivers stopped needing a metre of wire (computed from c/f/4) [verified]
- Combining the two, ExpressLRS's own numbers show band choice can be beaten by mode choice: 900 MHz 25 Hz LoRa (-123 dBm) versus 2.4 GHz 50 Hz LoRa (-115 dBm) is an 8 dB sensitivity gap that almost exactly cancels the 8.5 dB path loss penalty, so 2.4 GHz at the slowest rate is roughly equal to 900 MHz at its slowest rate in free space (derived from ExpressLRS firmware tables plus FSPL) [verified]
- DSSS spreads a low rate data stream with a much faster chipping code, and its interference rejection is quantified as processing gain Gp = 10 log10(chip rate / data rate); the FCC defines processing gain as the improvement in received signal to noise ratio from the spreading and despreading function (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2002/06/25/02-15951/spread-spectrum-devices) [verified]
- Worked processing gain values: 1 Mcps over 10 kbps gives 20.00 dB, 1 Mcps over 62.5 kbps gives 12.04 dB, 11 Mcps over 1 Mbps gives 10.41 dB, which is why 802.11b sat exactly at the old 10 dB regulatory floor (computed from the FCC definition) [verified]
- The practical split for RC: DSSS rejects broadband interference well and FHSS rejects narrowband interference well, which is exactly why Spektrum moved from DSSS-only DSM2 to DSSS-plus-hopping DSMX (https://sites.google.com/site/txwingsrc/links/rc-protocols; Horizon Hobby DSMX release 2011) [verified]
- Against the old bands the 2.4 GHz penalty is brutal: at equal distance and equal antenna gain at both ends, 2.442 GHz suffers 30.6 dB more path loss than 72 MHz, 36.9 dB more than 35 MHz and 39.1 dB more than 27.145 MHz (computed from FSPL) [verified]
- That penalty is conditional and often misstated: it holds for fixed antenna gain at both ends, because the receiving antenna's effective aperture shrinks as wavelength squared. With fixed physical aperture at one end the frequency term cancels, and with fixed aperture at both ends higher frequency wins (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-space_path_loss) [single]
- First Fresnel zone radius at the path midpoint is F1 = 8.656 sqrt(D_km / f_GHz) metres (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresnel_zone) [verified]
- Computed first Fresnel zone radii at 1 km: 5.54 m at 2.442 GHz, 32.3 m at 72 MHz, 46.3 m at 35 MHz, 52.5 m at 27 MHz (computed) [verified]
- The consequence is asymmetric: a 2.4 GHz model above roughly 6 m at 1 km has a genuinely clear first Fresnel zone, whereas a 35 MHz link would need 46 m of clearance and therefore never had one. The low bands always worked in a diffraction and ground-reflection regime, not a free-space one (computed; inference) [single]
- Low frequencies tolerate that obstructed regime because knife-edge diffraction loss scales with the Fresnel parameter, so the same obstacle costs far less at 35 MHz than at 2.4 GHz (https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-P.526/en) [single]
- ITU-R P.833-10 "Attenuation in vegetation", approved September 2021, covers 30 MHz to 100 GHz and is the authoritative foliage loss model (https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.833-10-202109-I!!PDF-E.pdf) [verified]
- ITU-R P.833-10 Table 1, from mixed coniferous-deciduous forest with mean tree height 16 m, gives specific attenuation of 0.04 dB/m at 105.9 MHz rising to 0.34 dB/m at 2117.5 MHz, roughly 8.5 times worse (same ITU document) [verified]
- The same table gives maximum excess attenuation of 9.4 dB at 105.9 MHz versus 34.1 dB at 2117.5 MHz, so vegetation costs about 25 dB more at 2.4 GHz once the path saturates (same ITU document) [verified]
- ITU-R P.833-10 also notes trees in leaf show about 20 percent greater specific attenuation around 1 GHz than leafless trees, and wind-driven foliage adds further variation (same ITU document) [verified]
- Quarter-wave element lengths across the whole history: 2.761 m at 27.145 MHz, 2.141 m at 35 MHz, 1.874 m at 40 MHz, 1.034 m at 72.5 MHz, 0.999 m at 75 MHz, 30.7 mm at 2442 MHz (computed) [verified]
- This explains legacy form factors: a 72 MHz receiver's roughly 1 m trailing wire was close to a real quarter wave, whereas the same wire at 27 or 35 MHz was electrically short and badly detuned, and the transmitter's telescopic whip was the only near-resonant element in the system (computed; inference) [single]
- At 2.4 GHz a quarter wave is 31 mm, which is what makes two-antenna spatial and polarisation diversity at 90 degrees practical inside a model, an option that did not exist at 72 MHz (computed; Futaba diversity documentation) [verified]
- 2.4 GHz introduced polarisation and shadowing sensitivity the low bands did not have, which is why manufacturers built prediction into the receiver, for example Futaba's Pre-Vision Diversity System (https://futabausa.com/futaba-rc-tech/air-radio-communication-systems/) [verified]
- Despite every propagation disadvantage, 2.4 GHz won on operational grounds the October 2004 Spektrum page states outright: no crystals, no frequency monitoring, worldwide ISM acceptance, built-in failsafe and a return data path (http://web.archive.org/web/20041018002205/http://www.spektrumrc.com/) [verified]
- Silicon economics mattered as much as physics: DSM used the Cypress WirelessUSB CYWUSB6935, a mass-market PC peripheral part, and FlySky's AFHDS 2A uses the A7105, so RC links rode a commodity cost curve that 72 MHz crystal gear never had (airplanesandrockets.com DX6 teardown; fareham.org.uk AFHDS 2A analysis) [verified]
- 47 CFR 15.247 governs both bands used by modern RC links: 902 to 928 MHz, 2400 to 2483.5 MHz and 5725 to 5850 MHz (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title47-vol1/xml/CFR-2023-title47-vol1-sec15-247.xml) [verified]
- FCC conducted power ceiling is 1 W for digitally modulated systems in all three bands, and 1 W for frequency hopping systems using at least 50 hopping channels at 900 MHz or at least 75 non-overlapping channels at 2.4 GHz (47 CFR 15.247(b)(1) to (b)(3)) [verified]
- A frequency hopper at 2.4 GHz with fewer than 75 non-overlapping channels is capped at 0.125 W, and a 900 MHz hopper with 25 to 49 channels is capped at 0.25 W, which is why hop-count claims in RC marketing are a compliance statement, not a performance one (47 CFR 15.247(b)(1) to (b)(2)) [verified]
- The 1 W limit assumes antennas of 6 dBi or less: above 6 dBi, conducted power must be reduced dB for dB by the excess gain, so a patch antenna ground station legally trades TX power for gain rather than stacking both (47 CFR 15.247(b)(4)) [verified]
- FCC dwell rules constrain hopping links directly: at 900 MHz with hop channels narrower than 250 kHz a system needs at least 50 hop frequencies and no more than 0.4 s average occupancy per frequency in 20 s, and the maximum 20 dB hopping channel bandwidth is 500 kHz (47 CFR 15.247(a)(1)(i)) [verified]
- The 500 kHz maximum hopping channel bandwidth at 900 MHz matches exactly what ExpressLRS uses for every 900 MHz LoRa mode, `SX127x_BW_500_00_KHZ`, so the regulation, not the radio, sets the 900 MHz data rate ceiling (47 CFR 15.247(a)(1)(i); ExpressLRS common.cpp) [verified]
- 2.4 GHz ExpressLRS modes use 800 kHz LoRa bandwidth, which is legal at 2.4 GHz but would exceed the 900 MHz hopping channel bandwidth limit, a concrete example of why the same firmware cannot offer 500 Hz on 900 MHz (47 CFR 15.247; ExpressLRS common.cpp) [verified]

## Unknowns / could not verify

- The January 2015 settlement amount in the Pirker case (commonly cited as USD 1,100) is not in NTSB Order EA-5730 and no primary source for it was retrieved.
- The Semtech part number inside TBS Crossfire. The TBS product page and manual never name LoRa or an SX127x part, so the widely repeated "Crossfire is LoRa-based" claim is not confirmed by any TBS document retrieved.
- TBS Tracer's exact launch date, packet rates and price. Only a late-2020 window from Wayback captures was established.
- TBS's legal founding date, co-founders beyond Raphael Pirker, and corporate location. Only the February 2011 website launch is documented.
- EzUHF's launch date and price, and Dragon Link's creator and launch date. Only a December 2011 EzUHF document and a 2012 Dragon Link V2 guide by Chris Seto were found.
- AlessandroAU's real name.
- The exact month and first product of Futaba FASST. Archival evidence bounds it to after May 2006 and before 2007-02-24 for the US market, but no Futaba press release or FCC grant date was located.
- FCC ID numbers and grant dates for early Spektrum, Futaba or FlySky modules. fccid.io returns 403 to automated fetches for those brands and fcc.report shows no matched filings under Horizon Hobby's FRN.
- Whether Nomadio or Spektrum was first to market on 2.4 GHz. Nomadio forum discussion predates Spektrum's October 2004 unveiling, but nomadio.com has no capture before 2007 and no dated Nomadio release was found.
- Nomadio's founders and what became of the company.
- Launch dates for Airtronics/Sanwa FHSS-1, Multiplex M-LINK, Graupner HoTT and Jeti Duplex.
- Release dates for the FlySky FS-i6 and FS-i6X. Secondary sources say 2014 and 2016 with no manufacturer or archival confirmation.
- Whether any manufacturer shipped PCM before Futaba, and the exact year of the Futaba FP-8SGAP. The candidate 1983 date comes only from a search snippet.
- The FCC docket number for the 1982 Report and Order that created the 72/75 MHz split.
- Confirmation that Cypress Semiconductor acquired Alation Systems' WirelessUSB line. Beard's AMA biography says he sold the line and the DX6 used a Cypress part, but the corporate link was not directly sourced.
- Any of the club-etiquette, flag-colour and PPM-shift-polarity claims against a retrievable primary document. They rest on search snippets of BMFA and club rulebooks.
- FrSky operating or selling product before its stated January 2010 founding, despite community claims of 2007 to 2008 activity. Earliest hard evidence is the 2010-01-04 FCC grant.
- The exact retail launch price of the original Taranis X9D in 2013. The verified USD 200 figure is the Taranis Plus at a US dealer in late 2014.
- A licensing dispute over closed hardware as a cause of the EdgeTX fork. The verifiable causes are stalled development, single-maintainer merge control, and Bertrand Songis moving to FrSky.
- Any quantitative market share figures for FrSky, RadioMaster, Jumper, TBS or ExpressLRS. No shipment or revenue data exists past FrSky's self-reported 2016 figure of 84 million RMB.
- Dated primary RCGroups threads. RCGroups serves a paywall interstitial to automated fetches (HTTP 402 via tollbit.rcgroups.com) and the threads tested are not in the Wayback index.
- Vendor announcement dates for the Jumper T16, RadioMaster TX16S, TX12, Zorro, Boxer and Pocket. The dates recorded are firmware-support and first-archived-page dates, which bracket the launches.
- Receiver street prices over time, in particular the frequently repeated "sub USD 10 ELRS receiver versus USD 40 to 50 Crossfire Nano" comparison. No archived retailer page was retrieved to fix either number.
- Whether ExpressLRS descends from any earlier codebase at all. The first commit is a self-contained ESP sketch, but no statement from AlessandroAU about prior art was found.
