# Origins and the Analog Era of FPV, roughly 1990s to 2013

Research notes. One claim per bullet, each with source URL and a confidence tag.

Tags: [verified] = two independent sources, or one primary source (company history
page, archived forum post, regulatory document, contemporaneous newspaper, patent).
[single] = one secondary source only. [lore] = widely repeated in the community,
not documented.

Research method note: the WebSearch tool budget for this session was exhausted
before work started, so all sourcing was done by direct WebFetch, including
DuckDuckGo HTML result pages and Wayback Machine captures. Some JS-only or
dead forum archives could not be reached, noted under Unknowns.

Anchor source used heavily below, cited as [RCFCA]: Wayne Andrews, "A History of
FPV", RC Flight Camera Action magazine, Spring 2017, pp. 16-25. PDF hosted by
CurryKitten. It contains direct interview quotes from Thomas Black, Denis Gratton,
Greg French, Raphael Pirker, Alex Greve, Martin Higgins, Simon Codd and Gary
Moscardini, so quoted statements from those people are treated as primary.
https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf

## Timeline

### Pre-history and lone experimenters, 1989 to 1999

- Carl Berry, an electrical engineer from Abilene, Texas and a former Boeing Aerospace employee, built "Project Cyclops", an RPV/UAV system that let him fly an RC plane from a ground cockpit using a video downlink. First test flight was in 1989. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- A contemporaneous newspaper article on Berry's system ran in the Abilene Reporter-News, Saturday 5 May 1990, headlined "This pilot does great flying without leaving the ground". The clipping is reproduced in the RCFCA article, which makes it the earliest dated press coverage of FPV flying located in this research. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Per that 1990 clipping, Berry was invited to speak at a convention of amateur radio operators in Dayton, Ohio about the invention, which the paper describes as using "amateur radio and television together", tying the earliest FPV directly to the ham ATV community. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Berry demonstrated Project Cyclops publicly at a sanctioned flying event in 1993 or 1994, flying from inside the enclosure of an old Sega arcade cabinet fitted with a 19 inch monitor and controls migrated from his Futaba radio. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The Cyclops airframe was an 8 ft long twin gas plane, 15 kg, 10 ft wingspan, sized that large specifically to carry a bulky CCD security camera. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Berry's ground station included a partial on-screen display: battery voltage and airspeed worked, altitude was not implemented. This is the earliest OSD in FPV located here. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Berry intended to sell kits based on Cyclops but never marketed it successfully, so the project stayed essentially unknown in the RC community. Carl Berry died in 2012. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- In 1997 Dave Upton, based in Oregon, produced and sold a video cassette instruction guide for the "INCAB-RC Videolink" system: a 900 MHz video transmitter and receiver set for RC planes, plus guidance on powering a TV and VCR in the field from a car battery and inverter. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Upton's 900 MHz system reached roughly 3 miles on a twin-engine gas plane, and he sometimes gained about another mile by having a person hold up a Yagi antenna and track the plane by hand, the earliest form of antenna tracking documented in this research. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Upton's video is the first documented instance of someone suggesting video goggles to improve video piloting. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Neither Berry nor Upton triggered wider adoption, and the RCFCA account attributes this to the absence of the distribution channels that later carried FPV: YouTube did not exist until 2005 and RCGroups.com is stated as not existing until 2001. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]

### The community forms, 1999 to 2005

- In December 1999 Thomas Black, known as "Mr RC-CAM", founded the RC-CAM group on MSN Groups, described in the RCFCA article as the first online community dedicated to getting wireless video back from a radio controlled plane. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Black's first project, RC-CAM1, used a monochrome camera and a 434 MHz video transmitter received on his home TV, weighed over 200 g, ran on 9 V alkaline batteries, managed roughly 150 ft of range with constant dropouts, and lasted only a few minutes. It was flown on a model helicopter and recorded to VCR. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Black's later RC-CAM4 project was based on the X10 "XCam2" consumer security camera, moving to 2.4 GHz video at about 5 mW, and the group collated how to repurpose that hardware for flying. This is the clearest documented instance of the security-camera-repurposing pattern that defined early FPV. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Black, interviewed directly, characterises the era as collective rather than inventive: "It was a slow but steady evolution. Development was driven by hobbyist that shared their success stories in forums; Significant online collaboration began in the early 2000's." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Black also documents the earliest FPV retail model: "Soon some small online FPV shops appeared (mostly operated by hobbyists with a regular day job) that sold ready-to-use wireless video equipment that came from the security camera market. These were often the same components that hobbyist had already evaluated and discussed in hobby forums such as rc-cam.com." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Val Petrov, handle "Cyber-Flyer", is credited with coining the term "FPV" around 2002 to 2003, replacing the then-current term "video piloting". https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Petrov built what the RCFCA article calls the first diversity receiver, self-made, and used it to claim 33,000 ft altitude within two years of his first flight, on a 7 ft wingspan plane with a 4-stroke engine whose mixture he could adjust remotely. He also claimed 5,000 ft with a helicopter. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The RCFCA article notes explicitly that at the time of Petrov's altitude flights "there weren't any legal guidelines in place stopping pilots from such altitudes/distances", which is the clearest statement of the early legal vacuum. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Denis Gratton started with an RC car in 1995 and began flying RC planes in 2001. His first video setup was a black and white camera about 2 x 4 x 1 inches at the pilot location, transmitting to UHF channel 14 of his TV, using a video sender intended for sending VCR output to a TV wirelessly inside a house. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Gratton's account gives a concrete example of the frequency-conflict problem: his 430 MHz video link interfered with the radio link of his RC car, causing a runaway, and he lived with that problem for six years until cheap 2.4 GHz video transmitters arrived. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Club-level gatekeeping existed early: in 2001 Gratton's local flying club required him to demonstrate three weeks of line-of-sight flying before allowing him to fly FPV. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]

### The breakout, 2006 to 2008

- In 2006 Denis Gratton, a French-Canadian in Montreal using the handle "VRFlyer", released a video of an FPV flight over the Royal Bromont golf club. The RCFCA article calls it the single video most responsible for pulling people into FPV. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- ImmersionRC's own company origin page states: "Since 2006, when vrflyer published his 'Bromont Royal Golf Club' video, the ImmersionRC team have been hooked on this rapidly developing hobby", corroborating the video's date and its role as the trigger for the company. Quoted in RCFCA from ImmersionRC's site. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Gratton discloses in interview that the Bromont video was edited to hide the true state of the technology: "the video transmission was poor with a lot a video interference, in fact, I kept only the good parts and cut everything else. But this is my secret and people thought it was perfect!" This is important: the video that recruited a generation misrepresented the reliability of 2006 analog links. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- The Bromont video's credits linked to the rc-cam site, and the resulting traffic spike crashed rc-cam.com several times within hours and cost Thomas Black "a small fortune in unanticipated bandwidth fees", per Black himself. This is a rare quantified adoption signal for 2006. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Gratton pushed head tracking on the forums for about two years with little interest, then sent his prototype module to another forum member who developed and sold head trackers commercially as RC-Tech, based in Switzerland, at about $300. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Anthony Cake, a co-founder of ImmersionRC, told Gratton the RC-Tech head tracker was far too expensive and sent him a prototype at $150 that was "far more advanced". Gratton's summary: "$150 and far more advanced...and ImmersionRC was born." This places ImmersionRC's founding impulse in head tracking, not video transmitters. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Greg French founded Fat Shark after trying to build a camera-based RC race car. In his own words: "My first experience was awful, which is why Fat Shark goggles were created. I was in China at the time cobbling together a hodgepodge of cameras, Tx's and Rx's that I picked up at the local electronic markets and using a pair of Oriscape goggles to drive an RC car. The system was so unstable and frustrating that I decided to put it all into one headset so I didn't have to deal with cables and connectors." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Fat Shark's first goggle product was the RCV130 vision system, aimed at RC car racing, not aircraft. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Fat Shark released the RCV922 in the summer of 2007, the first goggle designed specifically for the FPV pilot: 640 x 480 resolution, 46 degree field of view, 2.4 GHz receiver built in, bundled with a 50 mW video transmitter. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- French took pre-release RCV922 goggles to Canada for feedback from Gratton, who suggested adding a low-voltage alert, which went into the production models. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Raphael Pirker, later the frontman of Team Black Sheep, first heard about FPV from an Engadget article about Gratton: "I first heard about it on an Engadget article from a French Canadian called 'VRFlyer'. I was into model aviation back then, but FPV totally intrigued me." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Pirker's own description of his late 2007 start defines the state of the art: "We started in late 2007, using standard 35 MHz RC and 2.4 GHz for video, modules from security/surveillance systems that we soldered together. Goggles were hacked up ski goggles with displays inserted. In short, everything was very DIY." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Pirker's first FPV flight was on an F3J glider, 3.5 m wingspan, 1.5 kg all-up weight: "I got lost and flew 3 km away, before realising the entire day after, and flying back. I was sweating the entire day after, and didn't dare to fly for another week!" https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- A Brazilian pilot known as Gabriel flew proximity flights around Rio using baby monitor hardware as the video link, and is named by UK racer Martin Higgins as the first FPV pilot he watched. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]

### Antennas and the 5.8 GHz shift, roughly 2008 to 2011

- The typical analog-era station described for the mid to late 2000s was 35 MHz radio control with several metres of trailing antenna, and a mix of 900 MHz, 1.2 GHz and 2.4 GHz video, with 2.4 GHz emerging as the default. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- 5.8 GHz was initially rejected by the community. RCFCA: "Originally, you couldn't get people to touch it with a barge pole; expensive, power hungry, bad range, and most of all terrible multipathing issues." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The mechanism that forced the move to 5.8 GHz was the rise of 2.4 GHz spread-spectrum RC control radios, which collided with the then-standard 2.4 GHz video band. Pilots split two ways: move control down to 433 MHz long-range UHF and keep 2.4 GHz video, or keep 2.4 GHz control and move video up to 5.8 GHz. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The fix that made 5.8 GHz viable was circular polarisation. Antenna builders rediscovered the skew planar wheel, originally developed in the 1950s for very low-band radio, and scaled it down to 5.8 GHz wavelengths, greatly reducing multipath. RCFCA states this was a community effort, not one person's invention. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Alex Greve, handle "IBCrazy", is named as the most prolific of those antenna developers. He went on to develop the Cloverleaf antenna and founded Video Aerial Systems in 2011 to make these antennas. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Greve's route into FPV was via Pirker's videos and a pilot known as HappyKillmore: "How did I discover FPV? Like many people: Trappy's videos. Well, indirectly at least. Those got HappyKillmore interested in FPV, he let me fly his rig once and I was hooked." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Greve crashed on his first FPV flight, in HappyKillmore's plane: "After 10 minutes of cruising around his Easy Star in a park (nervously) the receiver locked up and died. Plane went nose-in hard. It is still on his shelf...broken." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]

### Multirotors displace fixed wing, 2009 to 2013

- Multirotors did not exist as an FPV platform in the early years: the small, cheap gyros and accelerometers needed did not exist yet, and pilots who wanted hovering flight used nitro helicopters. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- A commercial quadcopter, the Draganflyer, existed as far back as 1999 and was used in research at universities including MIT and Vanderbilt, but did not catch on in the wider RC community. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- In 2009 an RCGroups member from Massachusetts posting as "signguy" published a build thread showing how to build a tricopter. The thread grew to over 1000 pages. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- That original tricopter had no dedicated flight controller. It required a radio capable of 120 degree CCPM mixing and four separate helicopter tail gyros, with 10 mm square wood arms, a whittled plywood body, and a servo tilting one motor for yaw. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- David Windestal, a Swede posting as "dadde" and running RCexplorer.se, rebuilt signguy's tricopter with far better documentation and photography, and his first FPV tricopter flight is credited with cementing the tricopter as an aerial video platform. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Windestal's first FPV tricopter flight used a GoPro Hero 1 hacked to produce live video output, since live out was not originally a GoPro feature and the camera had to be fooled into thinking it was in playback mode. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- In late 2009 Rolf Bakke, handle "KapteinKuk", released plans for the KK board, the first cheap dedicated multirotor flight controller in this lineage: a PCB with 2 ICs, 19 capacitors and resistors, an LED and 3 gyros salvaged from the same tricopter-style gyros, with firmware written in AVR assembler supporting tricopter, quadcopter, hex and Y6. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The KK board had no flight modes and was tuned with three potentiometers using a screwdriver, and commercial ready-to-fly versions sold for around 80 GBP. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Bakke later worked with HobbyKing on the KK2 board, then fell out with HobbyKing, and HobbyKing released the KK2.1 on their own, after which the line "pretty much died off". https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The developer known as Alexinparis recognised that the Nintendo Wii MotionPlus controller contained a 3-axis gyro, combined it with an Arduino Mini and created the MultiWii flight controller, later adding the Wii Nunchuk accelerometers for auto-levelling. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The MultiWii code lineage is the direct ancestor of modern racing quad firmware: a developer ported it from the 8-bit Atmel chip to the 32-bit STM32 as Baseflight on a new board called the Naze32, which led to Cleanflight and then Betaflight. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- DIY Drones released the Open Source ArduCopter in 2010, adding advanced flight modes and autonomous flight, though it still required compiling code and flashing the controller. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- OpenPilot released the CopterControl board in 2010 with a friendly GUI for setup and tuning, but never manufactured enough boards to meet demand, with used boards reaching $300 on eBay. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- OpenPilot's successor board was the CC3D, and the project ended in August 2015, with developers forking it to LibrePilot. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- DJI's Naza v1 flight controller, sold alongside the FlameWheel F450 quad and F550 hex kits, is identified as the point where stable multirotor flight and aerial video became easy: pre-fitted motors and ESCs with bullet connectors, ten solder pads on the bottom plate, GUI configuration over USB and no compiling or special flashing software. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- A GPS add-on for the Naza gave GPS position hold and return-to-home failsafe, which turned the Naza into a controller that "really could fly itself". https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- DJI released the Phantom, a ready-to-fly quadcopter, in 2013. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- UK adopters Martin Higgins ("B4dlands"), Simon Codd ("Bignose13") and Gary Moscardini ("Shikra") are described as perhaps the first to FPV a tricopter in the UK. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Higgins on the DIY reality of early multirotor FPV: "Multicopter FPV, oh man, we had to make our own flight controllers with trimming pots on board! Buy the blank board, buy a Nintendo Wii remote, disassemble it and remove the gyro board and add it to the FC PCB. Auto-level never really worked, you would always be fighting to keep it in the air." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]

### Entry-point equipment of the era, pilot testimony

- Gary Moscardini started FPV in 2008 after seeing a YouTube video by accident, and notes that at the time "there were one or two guys in the world who were starting out selling parts from their homes". https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Moscardini's rig was 900 MHz gear added to a Multiplex Supercub, later with a Dragon OSD with return-to-home: "The gear worked well as long as you were away from any mobile phone towers." This is direct evidence of the 900 MHz versus cellular interference problem. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Moscardini also dates the arrival of commercial OSDs: "Just about then the first OSD's started to appear you could buy off the shelf. It made life alot easier but some were a little crude." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Martin Higgins taught himself to fly RC planes in 2010 specifically because he wanted aerial photographs, then found live video streaming instead, an example of aerial photography being the on-ramp to FPV. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- Higgins' first FPV flight used a 10 mW 2.4 GHz video transmitter with his first Fat Shark goggles strapped to a mini Showtime plane, got about 200 metres, and he bought a 500 mW 2.4 GHz transmitter "from some company in France" that same afternoon. The 10 mW to 500 mW jump illustrates the power escalation of the era. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- The standard airframes of the era are named as the Multiplex Easy Star glider, described as what "almost everyone was flying", and the Multiplex Twinstar. There were no purpose-built FPV planes, everything had to be hacked. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]

### Master chronology of dates that are primary-sourced

Compact index of the dated events this research could confirm from primary
records. Detail and sourcing for each sits in the thematic sections below.

- 5 May 1990: Abilene Reporter-News covers Carl Berry's Project Cyclops, earliest located FPV press. [verified]
- December 1999: Thomas Black founds the RC-CAM group, confirmed by the "©1999-2002" line on the archived site. [verified]
- January 2002: rc-cam.com documents four hardware generations, 434 MHz through the X10 XCam2 at 2.4 GHz, and names RCGroups as an established RC resource. [verified]
- 13 February 2004: RCGroups has 27,790 members and 1,786,370 posts, and "Mr.RC-CAM" is among the users online. [verified]
- 2006: Denis Gratton releases the Royal Bromont golf club video. [single]
- 3 December 2006: user bill_higgins posts a request on RCGroups for a video piloting forum. [verified]
- Last week of December 2006: RCGroups creates the "Video Piloting (FPV/RPV)" forum. Present on 31 December, absent on 24 December. [verified]
- Summer 2007: Fat Shark releases the RCV922, the first goggle designed for FPV. [single]
- Late 2007: Raphael Pirker begins FPV on 35 MHz control and 2.4 GHz video with hacked ski goggles. [verified]
- December 2008: RangeVideo is selling a complete FPV product catalogue and still has to explain on its homepage what FPV means. [verified]
- 2009: AMA Document 550 codifies FPV rules, requiring a spotter maintaining visual line of sight. [verified]
- Late 2009: Rolf Bakke releases the KK board plans. [single]
- 2010: ArduCopter released by DIY Drones; OpenPilot CopterControl released. [single]
- January 2011: ImmersionRC is selling head trackers, OSD and antenna tracking, and already frames telemetry as a regulatory compliance aid. [verified]
- First half of 2011: FPVLab launches. Placeholder page on 3 March, forum live by July. [verified]
- 2011: Alex Greve founds Video Aerial Systems to make circular polarised antennas. [verified]
- 17 October 2011: Pirker flies a Ritewing Zephyr around the University of Virginia campus. [verified]
- 10 October 2011: FPVLab has 1,393 members. [verified]
- 17 September 2012: RCGroups multirotor forum overtakes the FPV forum on both threads and posts. [verified]
- 5 December 2012: FPVLab has 6,709 members, up 4.8x in 14 months. [verified]
- Between 31 January and 5 March 2013: RCGroups promotes Multirotors from a subcategory of helicopters to a top-level site category. [verified]
- Between 5 and 31 March 2013: RCGroups promotes FPV to a top-level site category. [verified]
- 27 June 2013: FAA issues a $10,000 assessment order against Pirker. [verified]
- 2013: DJI releases the Phantom. [single]
- 10 December 2013: FPVLab has 16,213 members. [verified]
- 6 March 2014: ALJ Geraghty vacates the FAA's order, holding a model aircraft is not an "aircraft". [verified]
- 17 November 2014: NTSB reverses and remands, NTSB Order EA-5730. [verified]

## People

- Carl Berry, Abilene, Texas: earliest documented FPV builder, Project Cyclops, 1989 first flight, died 2012. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Dave Upton, Oregon: 1997 INCAB-RC Videolink instructional video, 900 MHz, first to propose goggles. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Thomas Black, "Mr RC-CAM", USA: founded the RC-CAM MSN group December 1999, later rc-cam.com; documented repurposing of the X10 XCam2 security camera for flight. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Val Petrov, "Cyber-Flyer": credited with coining "FPV" circa 2002 to 2003 and with the first home-made diversity receiver. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Denis Gratton, "VRFlyer", Montreal: 2006 Royal Bromont golf club video, the recruiting video of the era; long-running advocate of head tracking. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Anthony Cake, co-founder of ImmersionRC: built the $150 head tracker prototype that undercut RC-Tech and effectively started the company. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Greg French, Fat Shark, based in China: designed the RCV130 for RC car racing then the RCV922, the first FPV-specific goggle, summer 2007. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Raphael Pirker, "Trappy", Team Black Sheep: started FPV late 2007, produced the mountain proximity videos that recruited the next wave. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]

### rc-cam.com, the first FPV community site

- The January 2002 capture of rc-cam.com carries the copyright line "©1999-2002 RC-CAM by T. Black", independently confirming the December 1999 founding date given in the RCFCA article. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- rc-cam.com described itself in 2002 as explicitly non-commercial: "You will soon realize that this is NOT a commercial site. It is maintained by an R/C model pilot who believes that other hobbyists may have an interest in an affordable airborne video system." https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- The site's stated goal was cost, not immersion: it was about building "a very low cost video camera that could be carried by a small R/C model helicopter" because "the thought of buying an expensive commercial system wasn't very appealing". Early FPV was a cost-driven hack, not a sport. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- The 2002 site called the approach "a hacker's delight" and described the result as "a lightweight wireless color camera that is based on a small surveillance system", primary confirmation that repurposed security hardware was the foundation of the hobby. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- rc-cam.com's own generation summary is the clearest primary record of the early frequency progression, and is quoted here in full below. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- RC-CAM generation 1: "Off-the-shelf 434 Mhz wireless camera system. CMOS monochrome camera, sub-miniature video transmitter. Required HAM radio license, had poor video quality, and very short range." This is direct primary evidence that ham licensing was a real constraint from the very start, not a later debate. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- RC-CAM generation 2: "Modified CMOS color video camera and 2.4 GHz transmitter using a $88 consumer product. Very low cost, but the camera video would often wash out. Airborne transmitter was big and bulky." The $88 figure is a rare hard price point for 2000-2001 era gear. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- RC-CAM generation 3: "Based on RC-CAM2. Upgraded to a CCD color camera and eliminated most electronic modifications. Much improved video performance at slightly higher cost. Airborne transmitter still bulky." https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- RC-CAM generation 4: "Based on X10's tiny 'XCam2' camera system. CCD color camera. Small size and light weight (less than 6 oz) allows for installation on nearly any R/C model. Good performance." The X10 XCam2 was a mass-marketed consumer wireless security camera, and its adoption is the single clearest case of the security-camera-to-FPV pipeline. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- The 2002 rc-cam.com page lists the RC resource sites of the day as RunRyder, RCShowRoom, RC Online and RCGroups, confirming RCGroups existed and was significant by January 2002. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- rc-cam.com's live FAQ states why the site never sold aerial photography services, and in doing so documents the amateur-band legal constraint precisely: "our camera equipment operates on the Amateur Radio (ham) frequencies. In the USA, it is unlawful to use these frequencies for the gainful benefit of your firm/employer." This is the FCC Part 97 prohibition on pecuniary interest, stated by an early FPV practitioner in his own words. http://www.rc-cam.com/faq.htm [verified]
- rc-cam.com ran its own user-to-user forum with no membership fee, funded by private donors, with no banner ads or donations solicited, an important contrast with the later commercial forum era. http://www.rc-cam.com/faq.htm [verified]

### The Pirker case, the defining legal event of the era

- The FAA issued an assessment order against Raphael Pirker on 27 June 2013, seeking a $10,000 civil penalty for a violation of 14 CFR 91.13(a), careless or reckless operation. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The flight in question took place on 17 October 2011 at the University of Virginia campus in Charlottesville, using a Ritewing Zephyr, a flying wing that was a standard long-range FPV airframe of the period. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The FAA alleged Pirker flew at altitudes from 10 feet AGL up to 1,500 feet AGL, directly towards a person on a sidewalk who had to take evasive action, through a tunnel containing moving vehicles, under a crane, below tree top level over a walkway, under an elevated pedestrian walkway, and within about 100 feet of an active heliport. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- Critically for the legal analysis, the FAA alleged the flights were "for compensation", with Pirker "being paid by [a third party] to supply aerial photographs and video of the UVA campus and medical center". The commercial element, not the FPV element, is what brought the case. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- Administrative Law Judge Patrick G. Geraghty issued a decisional order on 6 March 2014 vacating the FAA's assessment, holding that the Zephyr was a "model aircraft" and therefore not an "aircraft" for the purposes of 14 CFR 91.13(a). https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The NTSB reversed that decision and remanded on 17 November 2014, served 18 November 2014, as NTSB Order No. EA-5730, restoring the FAA's authority over model aircraft as "aircraft". https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- The ALJ's reasoning relied on the fact that the FAA's only long-standing guidance for model aircraft was advisory circular AC 91-57 of 9 June 1981, plus a 2007 policy notice. A 1981 advisory circular being the governing document in 2014 is the clearest possible evidence of the regulatory vacuum FPV grew up in. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]
- Note the 20 month gap: the flight was October 2011, the FAA's assessment order was June 2013. Enforcement lagged the practice substantially. https://www.ntsb.gov/legal/alj/OnODocuments/Aviation/5730.pdf [verified]

### The US radio-law grey zone, from the regulations themselves

- FCC Part 15.249 governs unlicensed operation in the 902-928 MHz, 2400-2483.5 MHz and 5725-5875 MHz bands, and limits the fundamental to a field strength of 50 millivolts per metre measured at 3 metres. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-15/subpart-C/section-15.249 [verified]
- 50 mV/m at 3 m corresponds to roughly 0.75 mW EIRP, calculated from E = sqrt(30 x P)/d. A typical analog FPV video transmitter of the era at 25 mW, let alone the 500 mW and 600 mW units openly sold, exceeded this limit by well over an order of magnitude. The conversion is my own arithmetic, the limit is primary. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-15/subpart-C/section-15.249 [verified]
- FCC Part 15.247 does permit up to 1 watt in those same bands, but paragraph (a) restricts it to "frequency hopping and digitally modulated intentional radiators". Analog FM video is neither frequency hopping nor digitally modulated, so the high-power allowance was never available to analog FPV. This is the precise legal mechanism behind the grey zone, and it is why the hobby leaned on amateur radio licences instead. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-15/subpart-C/section-15.247 [verified]
- FCC Part 97.301 allocates amateur bands that map exactly onto FPV video bands: 33 cm at 902-928 MHz, 23 cm at 1240-1300 MHz, and 13 cm at 2390-2450 MHz. A licensed amateur could therefore run far more power than Part 15 allowed. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-D/section-97.301 [verified]
- The 1240-1300 MHz band has no unlicensed Part 15 allocation of the 15.249 type, so 1.2 and 1.3 GHz FPV video in the US required an amateur licence outright, unlike 900 MHz, 2.4 GHz and 5.8 GHz where a very low power unlicensed path at least existed on paper. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-D/section-97.301 [verified]
- FCC Part 97.113(a)(3) prohibits amateur transmissions "in which the station licensee or control operator has a pecuniary interest, including communications on behalf of an employer". This is why a ham licence solved the power problem but not the commercial-work problem, and it is exactly the rule rc-cam.com cited when declining paid aerial photography. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-B/section-97.113 [verified]
- Part 97.113(a)(2) separately bars "Communications for hire or for material compensation, direct or indirect, paid or promised". https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-B/section-97.113 [verified]

### AMA and the first formal FPV rules

- AMA Document 550, "Uncrewed Aircraft Operations Utilizing First-Person View", carries the copyright line "Copyright © 2009", establishing 2009 as the year the largest US model aviation body first codified FPV rules. https://www.modelaircraft.org/system/files/documents/550.pdf [verified]
- AMA Document 550 requires that "All FPV flights require an AMA FPV pilot to have an AMA FPV spotter next to him/her maintaining VLOS with the FPV sUAS throughout its flight", the rule that formally ended unrestricted long-range FPV within AMA-sanctioned flying. https://www.modelaircraft.org/system/files/documents/550.pdf [verified]
- AMA Document 550 requires FPV novices to be able to fly the aircraft without FPV first, and to fly a non-FPV test flight after installing a new FPV system or changing flight-critical systems. https://www.modelaircraft.org/system/files/documents/550.pdf [verified]
- AMA Document 550 acknowledges the licensing grey zone explicitly: "sUAS must operate on frequencies approved by the FCC for wireless video, radio control, and ground station telemetry systems. Some systems, because of power output or Amateur Band frequencies, will require FCC licensing". Note this wording is from the version updated 3 March 2021, so it cannot be assumed identical to the 2009 original. https://www.modelaircraft.org/system/files/documents/550.pdf [verified]

### Team BlackSheep

- Team BlackSheep's own about page dates its origin to 2007 and explains the name: Raphael "Trappy" Pirker "in 2007, was among the first in Europe to embrace FPV (First Person View) flying. While others flew with line-of-sight aircraft, Raphael donned goggles, earning his group the nickname 'The Black Sheep of the hobby' for their unconventional approach." https://testshop.team-blacksheep.com/about-us [verified]
- TBS itself treats the 2011 FAA case as formative: "Our commitment to pushing boundaries was tested in 2011 during a landmark legal battle with the FAA. This highly publicized case became a pivotal moment for the drone industry." Note TBS dates the episode to 2011, the flight date, whereas the enforcement action began in 2013. https://testshop.team-blacksheep.com/about-us [verified]
- Secondary sources give conflicting founding years for Team BlackSheep as a company, variously 2007, 2009 and 2011. The 2007 date refers to when Pirker started flying FPV, not necessarily to company formation, and no primary incorporation record was located. [single]

## Frequency bands: why each rose and fell

- The earliest experiments used 434 MHz, an amateur band, which rc-cam.com states outright "Required HAM radio license, had poor video quality, and very short range". Low frequency meant long wavelength, big antennas and little bandwidth for video. https://web.archive.org/web/20020121213810/http://www.rc-cam.com/ [verified]
- 900 MHz rose first for range: Dave Upton's 1997 system achieved about 3 miles, far beyond what 434 MHz managed. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- 900 MHz fell mainly because of cellular interference. Gary Moscardini, flying 900 MHz gear around 2008: "The gear worked well as long as you were away from any mobile phone towers." https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- 900 MHz also collides with the long-range control links that later occupied 868 and 915 MHz, so a pilot could not easily use 900 MHz for both video and UHF control. https://oscarliang.com/1-2ghz-fpv-guide/ [single]
- 1.2 and 1.3 GHz are the same band in practice, with usable channels roughly 1080 to 1360 MHz, and 1256 MHz and 1280 MHz became the most popular channels specifically because they are the only legal ones in the US, sitting inside the 23 cm amateur allocation of 1240 to 1300 MHz. https://oscarliang.com/1-2ghz-fpv-guide/ [verified]
- 1.2 and 1.3 GHz rose because they penetrated obstacles and delivered range that 5.8 GHz could not, and remained the long-range choice for years. https://oscarliang.com/1-2ghz-fpv-guide/ [single]
- 1.2 and 1.3 GHz fell for three compounding reasons: an amateur licence is required in the US regardless of power level and the band is illegal for hobby video in many countries, the antennas are physically large and absurd on a small quadcopter, and the band interferes both ways with 2.4 GHz control links. https://oscarliang.com/1-2ghz-fpv-guide/ [single]
- The interference mechanism is mutual, not one-directional: a high-power 1.2 GHz video transmitter can swamp the aircraft's own radio receiver and disturb GPS and digital servos, while a 2.4 GHz control transmitter desenses the 1.2 GHz video receiver on the ground. The community's fixes were antenna separation, notch and low-pass filters, and lower transmit power. https://oscarliang.com/1-2ghz-fpv-guide/ [single]
- The commonly repeated explanation that the second harmonic of 1280 MHz lands on the 2.4 GHz control band is not quite right: 2 x 1280 MHz is 2560 MHz, above the 2400 to 2483.5 MHz band. The practical problem is receiver front-end desensitisation and transmitter noise, not a clean harmonic hit. Treat the tidy harmonic story as lore. https://oscarliang.com/1-2ghz-fpv-guide/ [lore]
- 2.4 GHz rose as the analog FPV default in the mid 2000s because cheap consumer wireless video senders and security cameras shipped on it, which is what made FPV affordable at all. Denis Gratton's account is explicit that his six-year interference problem ended only when "a new, very good and cheap video transmitter hit the market: it was the 2.4 GHz video band". https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [verified]
- 2.4 GHz video fell because the RC industry moved control onto the same band. Once 2.4 GHz spread-spectrum radios replaced 35, 40 and 72 MHz control, video and control were fighting for the same spectrum on the same airframe. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- The band conflict split the hobby into two surviving configurations, both still in use: 2.4 GHz control with 5.8 GHz video, or 433/868/915 MHz UHF control with lower-band video. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- 5.8 GHz was initially rejected as "expensive, power hungry, bad range, and most of all terrible multipathing issues", and only became viable once circular polarised antennas suppressed multipath. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- 5.8 GHz then won decisively because its short wavelength allows tiny antennas suited to small multirotors, and because it is far enough from 2.4 GHz control to coexist on one airframe. https://oscarliang.com/fpv-frequency/ [single]
- European licence-exempt power limits shaped equipment design differently from the US: ImmersionRC in 2011 refers to "the 10mW 2.4GHz systems, and 25mW 5.8GHz systems common in Europe", which is why European pilots invested in directional antennas and antenna tracking rather than raw power. https://web.archive.org/web/20110131111548/http://immersionrc.com/ [verified]

## The transition: multirotors displace fixed wing

The best evidence located for this transition is structural rather than anecdotal:
how the largest RC forum organised itself, and when.

- Through 2011 the RCGroups FPV forum was overwhelmingly a fixed-wing space, and multirotors were not a top-level category anywhere on the site. https://web.archive.org/web/20110902000000/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/index.php [single]
- By 17 September 2012 the RCGroups "Multirotor Helis" forum carried 22,797 threads and 611,732 posts, against 17,831 threads and 582,975 posts in the "Video Piloting (FPV/RPV)" forum, and 30,431 threads and 387,333 posts in "Electric Heli Talk". Multirotors had overtaken FPV on both counts, and overtaken traditional electric helicopters on posts. https://web.archive.org/web/20120917000000/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/index.php [verified]
- The category name itself is the tell: multirotors were filed as "Multirotor Helis", a subcategory of helicopters, not as a platform in their own right. https://web.archive.org/web/20120917000000/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/index.php [verified]
- In the 31 January 2013 capture the RCGroups top navigation still had no multirotor entry, and multirotors were still labelled "Multirotor Helis". https://web.archive.org/web/20130131000000/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/index.php [verified]
- By 5 March 2013 RCGroups had redesigned its top navigation to "All, Planes, Sailplanes, Helis, Multirotors, Cars, Boats". Multirotors were promoted out from under helicopters to a first-class category between 31 January and 5 March 2013. This is the single most precisely dated marker of the platform shift found in this research. https://web.archive.org/web/20130305000000/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/index.php [verified]
- FPV was not in that early March 2013 navigation bar, and appears in it by 31 March 2013, giving navigation "All, Planes, Sailplanes, Helis, FPV, Multirotors, Cars, Boats". FPV was promoted to a top-level category about a month after multirotors were. https://web.archive.org/web/20130331000000/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/index.php [verified]
- The enabling chain behind the shift is well documented and consistent across sources: cheap MEMS gyros from Nintendo Wii hardware and dedicated boards (KK board late 2009, MultiWii, ArduCopter 2010, OpenPilot CopterControl 2010) made multirotors flyable, then DJI's Naza plus FlameWheel kits made them flyable without electronics skills, then the DJI Phantom in 2013 made them flyable without building anything. https://www.currykitten.co.uk/storage/2017/10/HistoryOfFPV_RCFCA11.pdf [single]
- Organised FPV racing, the thing that made multirotors the defining FPV platform rather than merely the most numerous, arrives after the period in scope. The Rotorcross club and brand in Western Australia states it was "Established in 2014". https://rotorcrossperth.livefpv.com [single]
- The French Airgonay group's forest FPV video, filmed in Haute-Savoie and released in October 2014, reached about 4.3 million views and is widely credited with popularising drone racing internationally. This is 2014, outside the analog-origins window, and confirms that racing was a consequence of the multirotor transition rather than a cause of it. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yMMDx6S2PV8 [single]

## Unknowns / could not verify

Things actively looked for and not confirmed. Listed so the gaps are visible
rather than papered over.

- The exact founding date and founders of ImmersionRC. The company's live about page returned HTTP 503 throughout this research, and the January 2011 archived homepage carries no founding date. Anthony Cake is named as "one of the co-founders" by Denis Gratton, but the other founders, the incorporation date and the country of registration were not established. The company's own site is quoted secondhand via the RCFCA article as saying the team was "hooked" since 2006, which is a start date for interest, not for the company.
- The exact founding date of RangeVideo and the identity of its founder. The December 2008 capture is a mature shop with no about or history page located, so RangeVideo was clearly trading before then, but no earlier capture was examined and no founder name was found.
- Fat Shark's 2007 founding rests on secondary sources plus Greg French's own interview account of the sequence of events. No company registration record, no Fat Shark-authored history page and no dated first-product announcement were located.
- Why FPVLab was founded, and by whom. The launch window is pinned to the first half of 2011 by Wayback captures, and the "FPV Without The Interference" tagline strongly suggests a split from an existing forum, but no founding announcement, no founder name and no primary statement of motive was found. The widely repeated story that FPVLab was a breakaway from RCGroups over moderation is recorded here as lore.
- RCGroups' own founding date. The RCFCA article asserts RCGroups did not exist until 2001, but the site was already large and long-running by the February 2004 capture, and RCGroups is generally described as growing out of the earlier Ezone electric flight magazine. No primary source for the founding year was obtained, and the 2001 claim should not be relied on.
- RCGroups sitewide membership after 2004. The February 2004 footer gives exact figures, but later templates dropped the sitewide member counter from the index page, so no membership growth curve for RCGroups could be built. Only per-forum thread and post counts were recoverable.
- Whether the "Multirotor Helis" forum on RCGroups was newly created or renamed from an existing forum. It does not appear on the February 2012 index capture yet shows 22,797 threads in September 2012, which is too large a jump for a new forum. The likeliest explanation is a template or nesting difference between captures, but this was not resolved, so no creation date for that forum is claimed.
- The original 2009 text of AMA Document 550. Only the version updated 3 March 2021 was retrieved, and the Wayback CDX queries for earlier PDF versions failed repeatedly under rate limiting. The 2009 copyright date is solid, the 2009 wording is not. AMA documents 580 and 590, referenced by 550 as covering FCC licensing, returned no content at the expected URLs.
- Val Petrov's coinage of the term "FPV" around 2002 to 2003 rests on a single secondary account. No archived forum post showing first use of the term was located, and the RCGroups forum name in December 2006 was still "Video Piloting (FPV/RPV)", which shows the term was current but not yet dominant by then. The coinage attribution should be treated as lore until a dated post is found.
- Val Petrov's 33,000 ft altitude claim is uncorroborated and comes from a single secondary source. No flight log, video, contemporaneous forum post or third-party confirmation was located. Given that 33,000 ft is above most controlled airspace floors and roughly airliner cruise altitude, this claim deserves scepticism and is flagged as lore.
- Carl Berry's 1989 first flight date rests on the RCFCA article. The reproduced Abilene Reporter-News clipping of 5 May 1990 independently confirms the project existed and was flying by 1990, but not the 1989 date specifically. The clipping's full text and byline could not be verified against a newspaper archive.
- No sales figures were found for any analog-era FPV company. No unit volumes, revenue, or shipment numbers for Fat Shark, ImmersionRC, RangeVideo or Video Aerial Systems were located in any source, primary or secondary. This is the largest quantitative gap in this research.
- No patent records were retrieved. Video Aerial Systems maintains a "Patented Innovation" page, but its content could not be extracted from the site's Shopify template, and no patent numbers for circular polarised FPV antennas were obtained.
- DIY Drones membership figures are weak. The 26,000 members figure for 2012 comes from a search snippet quoting Chris Anderson's Wired article, because Wired blocks automated fetching. The IEEE Spectrum figure of "more than 12,000 members" has no established date.
- The identity of "signguy", whose 2009 RCGroups tricopter thread ran past 1000 pages, and the thread's actual URL and post count, were not established. Likewise the real name behind "Alexinparis" of MultiWii and "HappyKillmore" were not found.
- The claim that Denis Gratton's Bromont video was covered by Engadget, which is how Pirker found FPV, comes from Pirker's own recollection. The Engadget article itself was not located.
- Contemporaneous evidence of the FCC actually enforcing Part 15 against FPV pilots was not found. The grey zone is well documented in the rules, but whether anyone was ever fined for an over-power analog video transmitter in this period remains unverified.

## Summary of what is solid versus what is soft

- Solid, primary-sourced: the 1990 newspaper coverage of Carl Berry, rc-cam.com's December 1999 founding and its four hardware generations, the RCGroups FPV forum's creation in the last week of December 2006 and its growth curve, FPVLab's 2011 launch and its 2011 to 2014 membership curve, Video Aerial Systems' 2011 founding, AMA Document 550's 2009 origin, the full Pirker case chronology, and the FCC rules that define the legal grey zone.
- Soft, single-secondary-source or self-reported: the 1989 Berry first flight, Dave Upton's 1997 system, Val Petrov's coinage and altitude claims, Fat Shark's and ImmersionRC's founding details, and most equipment specifications from the 2006 to 2008 period.
- Lore, repeated but undocumented: the tidy 1.2 GHz harmonic explanation, FPVLab as a moderation breakaway, and the framing of any single person as the inventor of FPV. The consistent testimony from participants, including Thomas Black's own words, is that FPV was a collective and incremental development, not an invention.
