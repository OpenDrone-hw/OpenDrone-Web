# FPV video systems and goggles: history research

Status: in progress. Research started 2026-08-12.
Convention: one claim per bullet, source URL(s), tagged [verified] (two independent
sources or one primary) or [single].
Research was split across two passes: digital, goggles, antennas and latency in the

## Analog era

### Cameras: the CCTV inheritance

- The single best piece of evidence that FPV started with security cameras: the PZ0420 user manual hosted on RunCam's own site is titled "2040 OSD Menu Manual" and carries the header "CCTV Cameras http://www.securitycamera2000.com". RunCam was shipping a rebadged CCTV board camera under its own brand. https://web.archive.org/web/20150505121422/http://www.runcam.com/download/PZ0420-User-Manual.pdf [verified as primary]
- RunCam's own authorised dealer list named securitycamera2000.com, kamera2000.com and camera2000.com, all CCTV retailers, confirming the supply chain rather than just the product. https://web.archive.org/web/20160315195249/http://runcam.com/index.php/support/about [single]
- RunCam sold the PZ0420H at USD 29.99 with 3.6 mm (67 degrees), 2.8 mm (86 degrees, plus USD 2) and 2.1 mm (110 degrees, plus USD 4) lens options, archived January 2015, and the product existed on runcam.com from at least 2014-04-06. https://web.archive.org/web/20150121025112/http://shop.runcam.com/runcam-pz0420h/ [single]
- Sony released the first Effio chipset (CXD4112GG plus CXD4813GG) in June 2009 explicitly for the security camera market. Every "600TVL Sony" FPV camera of the early 2010s is downstream of a 2009 CCTV product decision. https://www.videosec.at/data/uploads/Box/CXD4127(effio-e)+ICX-673AKA.pdf [verified as primary]
- Effio-E is a two chip set, an analog front end (CXD4816GG) plus a DSP (CXD4127GG), supporting 510H, 760H and 960H CCD sensors with built-in OSD menus in eight languages. Those OSD menus are the ones FPV pilots later navigated with a joystick on the back of the camera. https://www.videosec.at/data/uploads/Box/CXD4127(effio-e)+ICX-673AKA.pdf [verified as primary]
- The 600 and 650TVL numbers come straight from Sony: Effio-E with 960H CCDs (ICX672AKA, ICX673AKA) delivers "the high horizontal resolution of 650 TV lines or higher". https://www.videosec.at/data/uploads/Box/CXD4127(effio-e)+ICX-673AKA.pdf [verified as primary]
- The 700 and 800TVL generation is Effio-V (CXD4141GG) and Effio-A (CXD4151GG), which Sony specifies at "horizontal resolution over 700 TV lines, exceeding 650 TV lines of the existing Effio series". Effio-V also brought 3D-NR, WDR, defog and IR LED exposure optimisation, features that reappear verbatim in FPV camera menus. https://www.datasheethub.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/CXD4141-Datasheet.pdf [verified as primary]
- The Sony sensor branding ladder is HAD CCD, then Super HAD CCD II, then EXview HAD CCD II, the last defined by Sony as reaching 1000 mV or more per square micrometre sensitivity. https://www.videosec.at/data/uploads/Box/CXD4127(effio-e)+ICX-673AKA.pdf [verified as primary]
- TVL is not a trustworthy spec: it has "become less meaningful in recent years, as manufacturers often inflate these numbers as a marketing tool". https://oscarliang.com/best-fpv-camera/ [single]
- The measured penalty of the old CCTV board cameras is visible in the data: the PZ0420M averages 31 ms while the HS1177, also a Sony Super HAD II CCD, averages 19 ms, so "CCD" alone never guaranteed low latency. https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [single]
- CCD was chosen first and later overtaken: "CCD is an older technology and used to be the go-to image sensor for FPV cameras since it performed better than CMOS at the time... Modern analog FPV cameras almost exclusively use CMOS sensors". https://oscarliang.com/best-fpv-camera/ [single]
- RunCam was established in 2013, confirmed independently by its own About page and by the "Copyright (c)2013 RunCam Telecom Equipment Co., Ltd." footer on its 2014 homepage. https://web.archive.org/web/20160315195249/http://runcam.com/index.php/support/about https://web.archive.org/web/20140210002147/http://www.runcam.com/ [verified]
- The RunCam Swift shipped no later than March 2016 (shop page first archived 2016-03-25) and was explicitly built to the HS1177's physical size, sharing its Sony processing chip and sensor. RunCam's own manual gives 1/3 inch Sony Super HAD II CCD, 600TVL, 4:3, 12 g, 26x26x28 mm, 5 to 17 V, 0.01 lux at F1.2. https://web.archive.org/web/20161026043503/http://www.runcam.com/download/Swift-manual.pdf https://oscarliang.com/runcam-swift-fpv-camera-hs1177/ [verified]
- The RunCam Eagle is where FPV left the CCTV template: 1/1.8 inch 16:9 CMOS, 800TVL, Global WDR, 130 degree FOV in 16:9 and 140 in 4:3, product page first archived 2016-08-06 and marketed on "Widescreen 16:9". https://web.archive.org/web/20160928231908/http://www.runcam.com/download/Eagle_manual.pdf https://web.archive.org/web/20160806074503/http://shop.runcam.com/runcam-eagle/ [verified]
- The RunCam Eagle, the first widely adopted CMOS WDR 16:9 FPV camera, measures worse for latency than the CCD parts it replaced: 36 ms average versus 19 ms for the HS1177. The 16:9 and dynamic range win cost latency. https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [single]
- Foxeer was still a pure CCTV-derivative vendor in January 2016: its site listed only XAT600M, XAT600SM, XAT600, XAT650M and XAT800N, "Most popular Sony CCD 600TVL camera", with no Arrow. https://web.archive.org/web/20160109194136/http://foxeer.com/ [single]
- The Foxeer Arrow HS1190 (reviewed 2016-09-21) is the point where the OSD moved into the camera: Sony CCD 600TVL with built-in OSD carrying battery voltage on a dedicated VBAT pin, a timer and a pilot name, plus a microphone, about USD 40. https://oscarliang.com/foxeer-arrow-hs1190-fpv-camera-review/ [single]
- Foxeer product page first-capture dates: Arrow and Monster May to June 2017, Arrow Micro June 2017, Predator Mini November 2017, Falkor July 2018, Razer October 2019, Toothless February 2020. These are upper bounds on launch, not launch dates. https://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=foxeer.com&matchType=domain [single]
- Foxeer Razer sold at USD 19.90 for 1200TVL with 4:3 or 16:9 and lens choice made at purchase, and the Micro Toothless at USD 34.90 introduced the 1/2 inch sensor to the micro form factor. https://web.archive.org/web/20191017164121/http://foxeer.com/Foxeer-Mini-Standard-Razer-FPV-Camera-g-266 https://web.archive.org/web/20200206164941/http://foxeer.com/Foxeer-1200TVL-12-Sensor-Micro-Toothless-FPV-Camera-M12-Lens-g-271 [verified]
- The Caddx Ratel was covered by dated press on 2019-03-18 at 1200TVL, switchable 4:3 and 16:9, 0.0001 lux, and an "ultra-low latency of 8ms" vendor claim. https://www.fpv-report.com/2019/03/caddx-ratel/ [single]
- Caddx Ratel Pro (reviewed 2024-02-19) moved to a BSI 1/1.8 inch sensor at 1500TVL and 9.5 g, versus the Ratel 2 at 5 g and 165 degrees, showing the weight cost of the bigger sensors. https://oscarliang.com/caddx-ratel-pro-fpv-camera/ [single]
- The low-light "starlight" generation repeated the latency trade: Foxeer Toothless 2 Nano at 35 ms average and Runcam Phoenix 2 at 36 ms average, roughly 20 ms worse than the fastest analog cameras of the same era, such as Caddx Ant and Runcam Nano 4 at 13 to 14 ms. https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [single]
- Foxeer markets the Predator Micro at 4 ms latency, while the same camera family measures 15 ms average and 6 ms minimum on Oscar Liang's bench, so the vendor number is the best case minimum, not a typical figure. https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ https://firstquadcopter.com/news/foxeer-predator-micro-camera/ [verified as a discrepancy]
- Aspect ratio went from a separate SKU to a runtime toggle: the 2016 Eagle manual lists 16:9 and 4:3 as two distinct models, while by 2019 to 2020 Foxeer Razer and RunCam Phoenix 2 both list 4:3 and 16:9 as switchable on one part. https://web.archive.org/web/20160928231908/http://www.runcam.com/download/Eagle_manual.pdf https://web.archive.org/web/20200513044607/https://shop.runcam.com/runcam-phoenix-2/ [verified]
- Digital later reversed the aspect ratio trend: the Walksnail Avatar V2 uses "a native 4:3 image sensor instead of 16:9". https://oscarliang.com/best-fpv-camera/ [single]
- The sensor size ladder across the analog era is documented by the vendors' own spec sheets: 1/3 inch CCD on the 2016 Swift, 1/1.8 inch CMOS on the 2016 Eagle, 1/2 inch CMOS on the 2020 Phoenix 2 and 2020 Foxeer Toothless. [verified across the primary manuals cited above]

### 5.8 GHz band tables

- The canonical machine-readable band table is Betaflight's compiled-in default in src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c, band letters "-ABEFR", names BOSCAM A, BOSCAM B, BOSCAM E, FATSHARK, RACEBAND. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c [verified as primary]
- Band A (Boscam A): 5865, 5845, 5825, 5805, 5785, 5765, 5745, 5725 MHz. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c https://oscarliang.com/fpv-channels/ [verified]
- Band B (Boscam B): 5733, 5752, 5771, 5790, 5809, 5828, 5847, 5866 MHz. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c https://oscarliang.com/fpv-channels/ [verified]
- Band E (Boscam E): 5705, 5685, 5665, 5645, 5885, 5905, 5925, 5945 MHz. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c https://oscarliang.com/fpv-channels/ [verified]
- Band F, Fat Shark, ImmersionRC, also labelled Airwave in the TBS manual: 5740, 5760, 5780, 5800, 5820, 5840, 5860, 5880 MHz. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf [verified]
- Band R, Raceband: 5658, 5695, 5732, 5769, 5806, 5843, 5880, 5917 MHz. R7 at 5880 MHz is identical to F8, so the bands overlap. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf [verified]
- Raceband's 37 MHz spacing is uniform while bands A, B, E and F are not: band A steps 20 MHz downward and band B steps 19 MHz upward. Raceband is the only band designed for 8 simultaneous pilots rather than assembled from legacy products. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf [verified by arithmetic on a primary table]
- Raceband is ImmersionRC's, stated by ImmersionRC in the first person on 2015-08-12: video downlinks at 2015 race events "were based on ImmersionRC's 'RaceBand' channel set". https://web.archive.org/web/20151004061316/http://www.immersionrc.com/raceband-groundstations/ [verified as primary]
- Raceband did not exist in ImmersionRC's shipping transmitters as of December 2014 and January 2015: both the 25 mW and 600 mW product pages list exactly seven frequencies, 5740 to 5860 MHz, which is band F only. That bounds Raceband's introduction to 2015. https://web.archive.org/web/20150107183134/http://www.immersionrc.com/fpv-products/25mw-5-8ghz-av-tx/ https://web.archive.org/web/20141229213507/http://www.immersionrc.com/fpv-products/5-8ghz-600mw-av-tx [verified]
- ImmersionRC's later Raceband transmitters were sold as "15-Channel", which is the 7 band F channels plus the 8 Raceband channels, a detail that dates any given unit. https://www.getfpv.com/immersionrc-raceband-5-8ghz-600mw-a-v-transmitter.html [single]
- Band naming was already vendor-attributed by mid-2014: the TBS Unify 5G8 manual, revision 2014-07-11, labels its table "TBS Band", an unlabelled band B block, "Boscam / DJI Band E" and "Fatshark / ImmersionRC Band". https://web.archive.org/web/20150420160958/http://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify5g8-manual.pdf [verified as primary]
- "Band L" names two incompatible tables. TBS's own Low Race Band is 5621, 5584, 5547, 5510, 5473, 5436, 5399, 5362 MHz and is gated: "only available on special request (custom firmware for large events with prior legal body approval)". The widely circulated generic Band L is 5333, 5373, 5413, 5453, 5493, 5533, 5573, 5613 MHz. Do not treat the name as unambiguous. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf https://oscarliang.com/fpv-channels/ [verified that the published tables disagree]
- Neither Band L variant is in Betaflight's compiled-in defaults, which carry only A, B, E, F and R. Anything else needs a custom vtxtable. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/drivers/vtx_table.c [verified as primary]

### The bands that lost: 1.2 GHz and 2.4 GHz

- 2.4 GHz FPV video was mainstream commercial product into 2014 to 2015: ImmersionRC sold a 700 mW 2.4 GHz A/V transmitter at USD 69 in two SKUs, "One using frequencies legal for use by Amateur radio operators within the USA. Another for use in other countries with more flexible rules", paired with Fat Shark Dominators carrying a 2.4 GHz NexWaveRF module. https://web.archive.org/web/20141231192814/http://www.immersionrc.com/fpv-products/2-4ghz-700mw-av-tx [verified as primary]
- The structural reason 2.4 GHz video died is that it collides with 2.4 GHz control links, and ImmersionRC used exactly that to sell 5.8 GHz: "Not affected by 2.4GHz R/C radios!". https://web.archive.org/web/20150107183134/http://www.immersionrc.com/fpv-products/25mw-5-8ghz-av-tx/ [single]
- In the US the legal basis for 1.2/1.3 GHz and 2.4 GHz FPV video is the amateur service, not Part 15: 47 CFR 97.301(a) allocates 1240 to 1300 MHz and 2390 to 2450 MHz to Technician class and above. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-D/section-97.301 [verified as primary]
- HDZero's FCC-tested channels 5769 and 5806 MHz are Raceband R4 and R5, so HDZero deliberately sits on the analog race grid rather than defining its own. https://fccid.io/2A2O6HDZ3100 https://oscarliang.com/fpv-channels/ [verified]

### VTX control protocols: SmartAudio (primary source, TBS spec rev 09)

- SmartAudio is a TBS protocol using single wire half duplex communication over UART: both host and VTX hold the pin as input at idle, the host drives it to send a command, then releases it and the VTX answers. The VTX never transmits unsolicited. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- SmartAudio runs at 4800 bps with 1 start bit and 2 stop bits, logic high 0.9 to 3.3 V, logic low 0 to 0.5 V. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- SmartAudio frames are 0xAA 0x55 sync, one command byte, one length byte, payload, then an 8 bit CRC with polynomial 0xD5 over all bytes of the frame. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- TBS Unify Pro units with 5 V supply run SmartAudio V1. SmartAudio V2 was introduced with the Unify Pro HV, together with PitMode and CleanSwitch, so pit mode arrived as a V2 feature, not a V1 one. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- SmartAudio V1 sets power by writing the PA gain DAC directly, V2 uses a 4 entry lookup table (25, 200, 500, 800 mW), and V2.1 sets power in dBm using a level list the VTX reports in its Get Settings response. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- SmartAudio spec revision 09, dated 2018-11-01, is the revision that introduced V2.1. Revision 08 is listed in the same changelog as the "first public version" of the document. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- TBS opened the SmartAudio standard publicly but requests a license fee from anyone making money with it, with a logo program for licensees. Open documentation, not an open licence. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- The SmartAudio channel table is a single flat table of 40 entries, index 0 being Band A channel 1 and index 39 being Raceband channel 8, which is the canonical 40 channel analog map. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- SmartAudio V2.1 setting power to 0 dBm puts the VTX into pit mode, and pit mode changes take effect immediately without a power cycle, which was a V2.1 change. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]

### Pit mode (primary source detail)

- Pit mode is defined by TBS as an operation mode letting you power on the VTX without interfering with regular race operations. Fully supported only from the Unify Pro 5G8 HV onward. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- There are two pit mode variants: Out Range pit mode reduces output to minimum and moves the carrier to 5584 MHz, deliberately not one of the 40 popular FPV frequencies, while In Range pit mode reduces output to minimum on the currently active channel. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- Out Range pit mode was dropped from SmartAudio V2.1 onward: a V2.1 VTX asked for Out Range pit mode falls back to In Range pit mode. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- TBS names the product that introduced it: "A new feature introduced with the TBS UNIFY PRO 5G8 HV is PitMode", with both variants limited to 2 to 3 m range. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf [verified as primary]
- The functional purpose in TBS's words: "While in PitMode you can change channels and set power levels without affecting other pilots signal." https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf [verified as primary]
- ImmersionRC's equivalent is organiser-defined rather than fixed. The Tramp HV lists "Pit Frequency: User/Race Organizer Definable" and explains that the organiser sets it "to comply with local regulations", which is a materially different design choice from TBS hard-coding 5584 MHz. https://manuals.plus/video-transmitter/tnt-tramp-hv-5-8-ghz-video-transmitter-operator-s-manual.pdf [verified as primary]
- Both vendors' pit mode products date to the same window, mid 2016: the Unify Pro HV page is first archived 2016-06-09 and ImmersionRC's Tramp imagery is filed under wp-content/uploads/2016/06/. https://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=team-blacksheep.com&matchType=domain https://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=immersionrc.com&matchType=domain [verified]
- Betaflight treats pit mode as orthogonal to the power table: "Pitmode is separate from vtxTable. No power level should be created for pitmode", and notes SmartAudio V1.0 and V2.0 devices can only enter pit mode at startup. https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/guides/current/VTX [single]

### VTX control protocols: IRC Tramp, and when Betaflight got both

- The ImmersionRC Tramp protocol is a 9600 baud serial link using fixed 16 byte frames starting with 0x0F plus a checksum, driven half duplex on one wire via SERIAL_BIDIR. It is a different design from SmartAudio, not a variant of it. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/io/vtx_tramp.c [verified as primary]
- The power ladders differ too: Tramp is 25, 100, 200, 400, 600 mW while SmartAudio is 25, 200, 500, 800 mW. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/io/vtx_tramp.c https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/io/vtx_smartaudio.c [verified as primary]
- Tramp reports back band, channel, frequency, thermal protection state and RF power in mW, and carries a race lock flag (TRAMP_CONTROL_RACE_LOCK 0x01) so a race director can freeze settings. https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/guides/current/IRC-Tramp https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/io/vtx_tramp.c [verified]
- Betaflight autobauds SmartAudio from 4800 to 4950 in 50 baud steps, which is the firmware answer to TBS documenting plus or minus 5 percent software UART timing drift with temperature. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/betaflight/betaflight/master/src/main/io/vtx_smartaudio.c https://www.team-blacksheep.com/media/files/tbs_smartaudio_rev09.pdf [verified]
- Both drivers landed in the same window: first vtx_smartaudio.c commits 2016-10-10 to 2016-10-14, "Tramp prototype driver" 2017-01-09, both under jflyper. Betaflight v3.1.0, released 2017-01-25, is the first tagged release containing both; v3.0.1 of 2016-10-18 contains neither. https://api.github.com/repos/betaflight/betaflight/commits?path=src/main/io/vtx_tramp.c https://api.github.com/repos/betaflight/betaflight/releases [verified as primary]
- User-configurable VTX tables came later: the CLI vtxtable parser commit is 2018-12-19, vtx_table.c is in the 4.0.0 tree (2019-04-11), and Betaflight documents the user-facing change as landing in 4.1.0 (2019-10-16): "As of Betaflight 4.1.0, band/channel and power level information needed to control video transmitters is no longer hardcoded". https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/guides/current/VTX https://api.github.com/repos/betaflight/betaflight/releases [verified]
- Before serial control the fallback was physical, and ImmersionRC named the problem: race organisers avoided "something that racer's know as 'DIP Switch hell'" by handing out pre-set numbered transmitters. https://web.archive.org/web/20151004061316/http://www.immersionrc.com/raceband-groundstations/ [verified as primary]
- Button and LED menus persisted alongside serial control: the TBS Unify Pro manual documents a blink menu where 1x red is channel, 2x red is band, 3x red is power. https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf [verified as primary]
- ImmersionRC's alternative to a wire was contactless: the Tramp HV uses "Touch'N'Race (TNR)", described by ImmersionRC as "unique (and Patent-Pending)", letting a race director configure a powered-down quad's VTX with a wand. https://manuals.plus/video-transmitter/tnt-tramp-hv-5-8-ghz-video-transmitter-operator-s-manual.pdf [verified as primary]

### Power limits and the 25 mW story

- EU limit, primary text: ETSI EN 300 440 V2.1.1 (2017-03), Table 2, lists "5 725 MHz to 5 875 MHz / 25 mW e.i.r.p. / Non-specific short range devices". EN 300 440 is a Harmonised Standard under article 3.2 of Directive 2014/53/EU, so it is the CE route for a 5.8 GHz VTX. https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/300400_300499/300440/02.01.01_60/en_300440v020101p.pdf [verified as primary]
- US Part 15 limit, primary text: 47 CFR 15.249(a) permits 5725 to 5875 MHz at a fundamental field strength of 50 mV/m measured at 3 metres, harmonics at 500 uV/m. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title47-vol1/xml/CFR-2023-title47-vol1-sec15-249.xml [verified as primary]
- Converting that limit, 50 mV/m at 3 m is about 0.75 mW EIRP (P = E squared times d squared over 30). That is why even a 25 mW FPV VTX cannot be sold as a Part 15 device in the US. This is a derivation from the primary limit, not a quotation. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title47-vol1/xml/CFR-2023-title47-vol1-sec15-249.xml [single, derived]
- The US legal route is therefore the amateur service: 47 CFR 97.301(a) gives 5.650 to 5.925 GHz to Technician class and above. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-D/section-97.301 [verified as primary]
- There is no 5.8 GHz specific power cap under Part 97: 97.313(b) sets 1.5 kW PEP and 97.313(a) requires "the minimum transmitter power necessary". So "you need a ham licence above 25 mW" is a certification and market fact, not a numeric Part 97 threshold. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-97/subpart-D/section-97.313 [verified as primary]
- Manufacturers encoded the split in their own documents: TBS marks 25 mW licence free and 200, 500 and 800 mW as "requires HAM license to operate legally", while ImmersionRC sold 25 mW as "legal for license free use" and noted the 600 mW unit "May require a license to operate". https://www.team-blacksheep.com/tbs-unify-pro-5g8-manual.pdf https://web.archive.org/web/20141229213507/http://www.immersionrc.com/fpv-products/5-8ghz-600mw-av-tx [verified]
- Race practice tracked both regimes as early as 2015: ImmersionRC supplied "a 25mW license-free version, and for the US event a mix of 25mW (day 1), and 200mW (day 2) transmitters". https://web.archive.org/web/20151004061316/http://www.immersionrc.com/raceband-groundstations/ [verified as primary]
- FAI codified 25 mW in the Sporting Code Section 4 Volume F9 Drone Sport, 2019 Edition, effective 2019-01-01, clause B.1.5: "The VTX must be set with 25 mW maximum power emission." The edition's own amendment table lists it as new for 2019. https://www.fai.org/sites/default/files/documents/sc4_vol_f9_dronesport_19.pdf [verified as primary]
- FAI extended the cap to digital in the 2023 edition: "Analog and digital VTX must be set with 25 mW maximum power emission", plus a 25 Mbps constraint on digital. https://www.fai.org/sites/default/files/sc4_vol_f9_dronesport_23_0.pdf [verified as primary]
- FAI B.1.5 defers to local law rather than overriding it, and unauthorised VTX activation carries "a penalty going up to disqualification from the event". https://www.fai.org/sites/default/files/documents/sc4_vol_f9_dronesport_19.pdf [verified as primary]
- MultiGP's championship division requires "25mw Raceband video transmitters or HDZero video transmitters" for the National Championship, which is the clearest public statement of HDZero's race-scene status. https://www.multigp.com/rules/ [single]

### Analog's surviving niche, and the myth that props it up

- Two of the three reasons commonly given for analog surviving hold up: a complete analog setup can be under USD 100 with USD 15 replacement cameras, and 4 to 6 pilots fit comfortably on Raceband 1, 3, 5 and 7 without interference. https://blog.uavmodel.com/fpv-video-systems-showdown-analog-vs-dji-o4-vs-walksnail-vs-hdzero/ https://oscarliang.com/fpv-system/ [verified]
- The third reason does not hold up. The widely repeated claim that analog delay "from camera sensor to goggle display is under 2 milliseconds" is contradicted by the only large analog camera bench available, where the fastest cameras average 13 to 15 ms and the slowest average 37 ms, before the transmitter, receiver and display are added. The sub-2 ms figure is the transmission path only, mislabelled as sensor to display. https://blog.uavmodel.com/fpv-video-systems-showdown-analog-vs-dji-o4-vs-walksnail-vs-hdzero/ https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [verified as a contradiction]
- The measurement that actually settles the 2019 latency argument: DJI in low latency mode measured 15 to 27 ms on the same oscilloscope rig where analog measured 1.8 to 3.4 ms. Adding a typical analog camera at 13 to 15 ms average puts a good analog chain in the same 15 to 30 ms region as DJI's low latency mode. The skeptics were comparing DJI's whole chain against analog's radio link alone. https://www.facebook.com/groups/hdzero/posts/234332701928032/ https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [verified]
- What analog genuinely still wins is variance, not mean: analog latency was measured as consistent while DJI's was randomly varying across a 12 to 18 ms window, because DJI buffers a whole frame before compressing. Racers feel jitter, not average delay. https://www.facebook.com/groups/hdzero/posts/234332701928032/ [single]
- HDZero's design answer to that is block-wise transmission rather than whole-frame buffering, giving a latency that ramps predictably within a frame, 3 to 19 ms and slowly increasing, instead of jumping around. This is the real technical argument for HDZero in racing, and it is supported by both the scope measurements and Divimath's own datasheet. https://www.facebook.com/groups/hdzero/posts/234332701928032/ https://fccid.io/2A2O6HDZ3100/User-Manual/User-manual-5456018.pdf [verified]

## Antennas and RF

- The cloverleaf was published to the FPV community on 2011-02-11 at 19:23, post 1 of RCGroups thread 1388264, "Build IBCrazy's Cloverleaf: The ultimate circularly polarized aerial antenna!", by IBCrazy, real name C. Alex Greve, of Amherst, Virginia, RCGroups member since June 2006, amateur callsign KJ4RIV. Retrieved from the Wayback Machine copy of the thread. https://web.archive.org/web/2012/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?t=1388264 [verified as primary]
- Greve's own description of the design in that post: the cloverleaf is derived from the skew planar wheel with one lobe removed and the remaining three arranged at 120 degrees, which buys better SWR. He measured 1.0 SWR on his first build versus 1.2 as the skew planar wheel's best. https://web.archive.org/web/2012/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?t=1388264 [verified as primary]
- Greve's own numbers for the cloverleaf: gain only 1.2 dBi, which he calls ideal for an airborne antenna, and reverse polarization rejection that he describes as "very erratic", from minus 8 dBi to 19 dBi depending on how the signal arrives. The multipath rejection benefit is therefore highly geometry dependent, which is not how it is usually retold. https://web.archive.org/web/2012/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?t=1388264 [verified as primary]
- The design is a derivative, not a clean-sheet invention. Greve credits Michael, handle Mictronics, with modelling it in 4NEC-2, and positions the cloverleaf explicitly against the pre-existing skew planar wheel. https://web.archive.org/web/2012/http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?t=1388264 [verified as primary]
- Greve released the build as a free gift to hobbyists while explicitly reserving commercial rights: "please do not market and sell this antenna without my (C. Alex Greve) expressed permission... Competitors please invent your own just like I did." The assembled commercial version is the BlueBeam, built by him and his team. ## Latency numbers found (methodology per number)

### Method notes, read before using any number

- Vendor latency claims and independent measurements are not the same quantity and are routinely conflated. DJI's 28 ms, HDZero's sub-3 ms and Walksnail's OSD readout are all vendor or device-reported figures with different definitions of the endpoints. Only Oscar Liang's camera bench and Chris Rosser's high speed camera work below give a defined method.
- Oscar Liang's analog camera method: the camera is connected directly to a display over AV, an Arduino switches an LED in front of the camera, a phototransistor watches the display for the brightness change, the Arduino samples at 10 kHz, and 500 random measurements are taken per camera. Published May 2017, updated May 2021. This measures camera plus display, not the radio link. https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [verified]
- Because analog video is a continuously scanned raster with no frame buffer, a single camera does not have one latency: it has a range set by where in the field the event lands. Oscar Liang's data shows every camera as a min, max and mean, and the spread is typically 15 to 20 ms wide. Any single-number analog latency claim is hiding this. https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [verified]
- Chris Rosser's method: a 960 fps high speed camera films the source and the goggle display together, and he separates fixed versus variable latency, pixel switching time and screen refresh behaviour rather than quoting one number. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9SBc35SccDg [single]

### Reconciling the two analog numbers

- The Quellet and Kubascik analog figure of 1.8 to 3.4 ms cannot be the same quantity as Oscar Liang's analog camera figures of 13 to 37 ms average, because the camera sensor readout alone exceeds their whole number. Their measurement is the transmit, receive and display path with the camera's own capture delay excluded or bypassed, despite being labelled glass to glass. Do not quote 3 ms as analog end-to-end latency. https://www.facebook.com/groups/hdzero/posts/234332701928032/ https://oscarliang.com/fpv-camera-latency/ [verified as a contradiction]
- Their architectural explanation is the useful part and matches the Divimath datasheet: analog sends video line by line continuously, HDZero processes in blocks in the manner of wireless HDMI, and DJI captures a whole frame before compressing and sending it as a block. That is the real reason DJI latency varies randomly while HDZero latency ramps within a frame. https://www.facebook.com/groups/hdzero/posts/234332701928032/ https://fccid.io/2A2O6HDZ3100/User-Manual/User-manual-5456018.pdf [verified]
